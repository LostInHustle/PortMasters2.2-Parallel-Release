# PortMasters 2.2 Parallel Release: System Analysis

## Origin and Lineage

PortMasters 2.2 Parallel Release is a browser based multiplayer trading game set on the ancient maritime Silk Road. The original prototype was a single player HTML build titled PortMasters: Lords of the Silk Road. The Parallel Release is the third branch, written by Joe Zhou and Aaron Zhu as a Next.js 16 application that takes the single player game online. One process serves the site, the API, and the realtime Socket.IO layer on a single port.

The verbatim economy (Hemp, Silk, Tea, the founding ports, the all or nothing pirate raid, the 10 percent escort fee) is preserved from the original single player build. The Parallel Release layers on top of it a deterministic multiplayer engine, a persistent Captain's Legacy, a social economy of barter and loans, and a three tier difficulty framework.

The 2.2 build branches from [PortMasters 2 Parallel Release](https://github.com/LostInHustle/PortMasters2-Parallel-Release), the earlier multiplayer build, which is where the deterministic engine, the ready check, the three difficulty tiers, the social economy and the Ledger Integrity Pass were designed. Everything that build shipped is still here, working the same way. `docs/RELEASE_NOTES.md` sets the two side by side and walks through the six systems 2.2 adds.

## The Game Loop

Every captain in a harbor plays the same voyage in lockstep. Nobody advances a phase until every still active captain has readied up, or until the phase's own budget runs out, which since [B2] announces the same advance and leaves a captain who had not acted to the phase's defaults. A round runs through the six phases of the leg, in the order the design names them (`LEG_PHASE_ORDER`, `src/lib/game/phases.ts`):

1. Dawn. Draw from a fresh pool of boons that bend the rules for the coming round.
2. Market. Buy raw materials from the port market, then hire artisans and assign what each of them crafts. Two stations under one gate: the artisan bench sits inside the market because it is one captain's turn at the harbor rather than a second step the room waits on.
3. Orders. Fill trade orders for Gold and Reputation.
4. Parley. Trade goods and Gold directly with the other captains on the Captain's Exchange, which is open to every captain at any Renown level, and hold the two votes a Gambit harbor gets.
5. Resolve. Production lands, wages and maintenance come due, pirates may find you.
6. Dusk. Upgrade the ship, draft and rig modules, and close the round into the next one.

The phase union encodes every phase a captain can be in, and the six above are the ones the room synchronizes on:

```typescript
type Phase =
  | "harbor" // the pier, before the leg begins
  | "dawn" // the boon draft
  | "market" // port purchase, then the artisan bench
  | "orders" // trade orders
  | "parley" // the Captain's Exchange, and a Gambit harbor's votes
  | "resolve" // production, wages, maintenance, pirates
  | "dusk" // shipyard, and the round's close
  | "module_draft" // personal sub state
  | "module_swap" // personal sub state
  | "bankruptcy" // terminal in Classic, a mark elsewhere
  | "endgame"; // terminal
```

Which order a mode walks its six in is that mode's lap, and the lap is written on the mode record (`checkpointPhaseOrder` in `src/lib/game/mode.ts`, the one place an order is written down) rather than in the checkpoint module, because the two modes do not walk the same one and that difference belongs beside every other difference between the voyages. Classic runs Dawn, Market, Parley, Orders, Resolve, Dusk, which sorts the goods before it sorts the conversation. Ocean Gambit runs Dawn, Market, Orders, Parley, Resolve, Dusk, which closes the manifest before the table opens. Both laps open at the pier, visit each of the six leg phases exactly once, and close at Dusk, so a round closes the same way in either; the modes disagree about nothing but where the Parley sits. The personal sub states and the terminals never become room checkpoints.

The phases were numbered once, and a mixture of numbers and strings is what the leg grew one insertion at a time. Six named phases replaced all eight checkpoints in [B1], so the older values are gone rather than kept beside the new ones: a lap written twice is the failure the six exist to remove. What survives is a migration table, `LEGACY_PHASES` in `src/lib/game/phases.ts`, which maps every value the engine has ever persisted to what it is called now, read by `normalizePhase` on every load. A save or a room row written before the release keeps sailing, and the fold is recorded there rather than left to be rediscovered: bartering became Parley, artisan management became Market.

The lap is written down once and briefed once, and the briefing is the same record (`briefing` in `src/lib/game/mode.ts`). Which shape it takes is a property of the mode rather than a choice the Welcome screen makes, and the record says so in its own type: Classic briefs in a line and Gambit briefs in a chart. The line is prose rather than a fold over the phase order, because the lap counts seven entries and a captain pays for six of them: the harbor is a step nobody takes, and the line names the six legs in the order this mode walks them. That is what makes the two shapes one briefing rather than two: both are renderings of the same lap, and a mode whose line named a phase its lap does not run, or named them out of order, would be briefing a voyage nobody sails. Both briefings are written to be read side by side, so the legs the two modes share are worded the same way and only the legs the mode actually moves change their words, which leaves a captain who has sailed both with the mode's difference and nothing else. How many legs there are is said inside the briefing and nowhere beside it. The Welcome screen used to print a count of its own above the line, and it read four while the chart it was introducing listed five, so the pill's headline contradicted the description it was introducing, on one row of one panel. Both records count the same six today, and the count stays gone for the reason every second copy in this tree is gone: nothing printed beside a briefing gets to be a second answer to how many legs it has.

A chart is not a decorated sentence. It is the same legs as data: each one names the phase it is, carries an icon and the fill of that phase, and prints two lines, what happens there and what it decides for the legs after it. The second line is the reason the mode briefs this way at all, because the whole argument of Ocean Gambit is an order, the manifest closing before the table opens, and an order is the thing a chart shows and a sentence makes a reader reconstruct. It also prints what the line compresses: the boon draft the line names in a clause, and the artisans, who are a station inside the market in both modes, and it draws the run between them and the loop back to the top. The rail is rendered by `RoundFlow` on the Welcome screen with the first leg open and any leg focusable, so the reading moves back and forth along the round rather than once down it; every leg's body is always shown and only the consequence line is the one the focus picks, because a chart nobody clicks still has to be readable. The smoke suite is what holds either shape where it cannot rot: a mode's own copy is swept for the dash characters the house style bans and for empty strings, a line is read off the arrows it prints and is checked for naming each leg of its lap once, in the order the engine walks it, and a chart is checked the same way off its own leg list. Both are then checked against the lap for the one move the modes disagree about, the position of the manifest against the table, read off the words and off the lap separately so that a briefing stating it backwards cannot pass while the engine runs the right order.

A mode's rules reach a captain through five surfaces, and they are one record read five times rather than five documents about one mode. The record carries two fields beside the briefing for exactly this: `failureRule`, one sentence for what happens to a seat whose books fail, and `differences`, the list of what a mode changes against the voyage every other mode is measured by, which Classic leaves empty because it is the voyage the others are measured against. The surfaces are the tutorial a crew opens from the Welcome screen, the guide and the advice behind F1, the manual in the lobby, and the Welcome panel itself, and each of them prints those fields rather than restating them, so a rule retuned in the record arrives on every surface at once and no surface can teach a rule the record does not hold. The guide and the advice keep their own structure, which is why they print the record's sentences inside a shape of their own rather than the record wholesale: the guide names the mode, quotes its tagline, states its failure rule and lists its differences above an objective that counts the voyage's rounds, and the advice heads itself with the rule that applies to the seat it is advising, because every line under that heading is written for a captain whose books can sink them. The manual carries the same list on a page of its own, second, directly after the harbor, since a captain opening a manual before their first voyage is asking what this voyage is before they ask how a phase works.

The defect those five were arranged around is worth naming, because of how long it lived and how little of the tree could see it. The tutorial drew Classic's lap by hand and promised, on its second page, that failing the bills ends the voyage, which is the one thing the mode built on the opposite pillar does not do: a captain who picked Ocean Gambit read that their seat was gone, and the mode's whole design rests on the opposite, that a captain whose books fail keeps their card, their vote and their say at the table. The guide's phase list was a fourth drawing of the same lap, so a mode that moves the manifest ahead of the table was briefed with the order that does not, and the manual ended its settlement page on the founding rule as well. Nothing in the tree was lying: each surface was accurate about Classic, and the mode record had no field for the answer, so every surface answered the question with the only voyage it knew. The fields are what close it, and the smoke suite is what holds them closed. It reads each of the surfaces for both modes at every tier and checks three things: that the record's own sentences arrive intact on the screen, that Gambit is documented differently from Classic on all three of the surfaces that take a mode, and that the words actually printed carry no dash of any kind, which is read with the markup stripped so that a style attribute's hyphens are not mistaken for a sentence's. Two of the five are components rather than functions and are read as source instead, with their comments taken out: the check is about what a screen prints, and this tree's comments quote the sentence they replaced, which is what makes a repair readable a year later. The copy counts rounds throughout, because a voyage is the whole run and a round is one lap, and the Welcome screen had greeted every crew with a number of voyages where the voyage itself was the thing being counted.

One thing Ocean Gambit draws is not per captain at all. The voyage's public objective comes from a harbor wide seed, `${harborId}:V${voyageEpoch}:objective`, the same shape the market and the order board use, and it is the harbor rather than the captain that is in it. Every captain computes the commission locally from values they already hold and they all reach the same answer, which is why the server never has to tell anyone what it is, and why the epoch is enough to reroll it when the host restarts the voyage.

The quota rung is the one adjustment that seed carries beyond the harbor. A fleet of five is asked for a quarter again as much of every good, and a fleet of six or more for half again, rounded up per good (`SEAT_BANDS` in `src/lib/game/objectives.ts`). The entry the harbor drew does not change, only the amounts it asks for: a table of six draws the same commission a table of four would have drawn, and works a larger version of it. The rung exists because the sabotage window has to stay the same width at every table size. The Pirate's whole game is keeping the board from closing, and the hands available to close it multiply with the seats, so a board that did not grow with the fleet would make the traitor's job easier at five seats than at four and easier again at six, and the mode's win rates, which are targets rather than observations, would swing with the size of the room. Widening the board rather than the crew keeps the pressure on the fleet the same and lets the targets be stated for the mode instead of for each table size.

Which rung a voyage sails on is fixed at departure, not read from the roster. The room records the count that set sail in `Room.voyageSeats`, written in the same update that starts the voyage and cleared to zero in the one that restarts it, and the board the server deals and clamps against is drawn from that column. The seed reflects it as well, and only above the founding band: the suffix `:S${seats}` is appended at five seats and up, so a voyage of four or fewer, or one whose pin is missing because it began before the rung existed, seeds exactly the string it always seeded and draws exactly the board it always drew. A client is told the number twice, on the departure frame that moves the lobby into a voyage and with the save when it asks for the game state, because it is the one room fact a client cannot work out for itself: the roster it can see drifts as captains come and go, and the board the fleet is working on must not. Until the room has answered, a client draws the founding board and keeps the delivery button shut rather than absent, since goods handed over against the wrong board would report a delivery against a quota nobody set. `src/lib/game/balance.ts` reads how the rung actually performed: it groups the conclusions H4 began recording into a role by table size grid and judges each cell against the mode's targets, with the unplayed cells reported as unknown rather than as a rate of zero, and `npm run report:bands` prints it over the live chronicle. One consequence travels further than the board: `widestObjectivePayout()` spans the widest band rather than the authored deck, so the Ledger Integrity Pass ceiling and the peer trade ceiling derived from it follow a wider commission automatically instead of reading a real payout as a forgery.

The Manifest Audit is the mode's one evidence tool, and the only decision in the game that does not have to be unanimous. From leg five, once a voyage, more than half of the captains still sailing may nominate one captain during the Parley checkpoint, and the harbor is shown a sample of what that captain filed: two of their last five order fulfillments, drawn at random, and nothing else. The randomness is the design rather than a shortcut. Two of five means an innocent captain can look terrible by chance and a guilty one can come up clean, so the table argues about what it saw rather than accepting a verdict, which is the distance between this and the seer role it is modeled on. The vote is counted against the same active roster every advance already waits on (`src/server/realtime/audit.ts`), so a bankrupt captain is not a vote the harbor is waiting on, and it may be called once a voyage. It costs the leg's Parley: the fleet pays the rest of a leg's trading for a partial answer, and that price is real rather than decorative. The cost is not paid by the checkpoint. The server tells the room what it found and each client leaves the phase the way it leaves every phase, by marking ready, because a server that emptied the checkpoint's ready set on the room's behalf would move the checkpoint while every client sat waiting to be told to move, which is the one way to leave a harbor stuck. A captain who comes back after the vote is handed the finding directly, the same way a joiner is handed the commission's board, and the once a voyage limit is the reveal itself rather than a second flag beside it, which is why restarting the voyage clears it: a new voyage that inherited the old finding would have spent its own audit before it began. What opens is drawn from the seed `${harborId}:V${voyageEpoch}:audit:R${round}:${targetUserId}` (`src/lib/game/audit.ts`), so the server is the only party that can read the manifest but any captain holding it could work out the identical two lines, which is what makes a reveal checkable rather than trusted. The number of lines revealed is the plan's tuning knob and is a named constant in that module, because two of five and three of five are entirely different games. One clause of the plan is not in it: the audit was also to show the audited captain's current Larder, and the Larder is epic C1, unbuilt, with no food, no stores and no upkeep anywhere in a save. The clause lands with C1 rather than putting a number on screen that no rule ever moves.

The harbor's heavier vote is the maroon, and the pair is the point: a harbor that can open a manifest at a simple majority cannot necessarily put anyone ashore, and the arithmetic is where that shows. From leg nine, once a voyage, two thirds of the captains still sailing may name one captain to put ashore, counted against the same active roster the audit counts against, so a nomination from outside the room's own arithmetic never moves it. The comparison is inclusive rather than strict, and that is the only reading that means anything at the sizes a harbor is dealt: strictly more than two thirds of a table of three is three votes, which is unanimity and therefore not a vote at all. A marooned captain is not eliminated, and that is the reason the mechanic exists. The harbor takes the ship with everything bolted to it and everything in its hold, and half their Gold, and the captain keeps the rest of the purse, their crew, their standing at the table, their card and their seat, because the mode is built on a table that cannot shrink and on what a room does with a captain it can see and does not trust. The arithmetic lives in `src/lib/game/maroon.ts` so it can be tested without a socket, and the vote itself sits next to the sockets that need it in `src/server/realtime/maroon.ts`, on the same pattern the audit set: the room's facts come from the room rather than from the payload, so a client cannot call a vote in a harbor playing Classic or name a port the charter has not opened. What the vote buys is the Harbormaster's hand, the one piece of another captain's state a client is allowed to move. Once a leg, the captain the harbor put ashore names one port the charter has opened by the leg the market lands on, and every raw goods price at that port is leaned a tenth up or a tenth down, in front of the whole table. It is held by the server for the leg it was called in and delivered on the advance that opens the next market, exactly as the Harbor Pulse is, and the rule that delivers it is the rule that expires it: a market two legs later is not a market the harbor is still under, so a hand that is not sent again is a hand that has to be taken off. A call made in the closing leg is refused rather than held, because it would lean a market that never opens, which is also why the console is hidden there. A client stamps the delivered call onto its own voyage state and prices the market against it through `portShiftMultiplier`, so a leaned market is one more hand on the same price rather than a second pricing path, and it keeps the floor at one Gold and the pulse's own nudge in one rounding; product cards are priced from their recipe and are never leaned. Two of the mode's own fields carry all of this, and neither is a special case at the reader. `maroonFrom` is the rung, on the mode record beside the other rule that decides a seat's fate, and `bankruptcyIsFinal` is that other rule: true for Classic, where insolvency still ends the voyage exactly as it always did, and false for Ocean Gambit, where the same failure writes the mark and leaves the captain sailing to the endgame screen with it. That split is why the phase union's bankruptcy member is a terminal state in one mode and only a value nothing writes in the other, and why the mark is read from the status broadcast rather than from the phase: a screen badges a captain from the flag, a late joiner is hydrated from the same frame, and the server refuses a nomination aimed at a captain whose race is already run, all from one reading of one rule (`src/lib/seatMarks.ts` on the client, and the room's own cache of that status on the server).

A harbor's one locked door is a room setting rather than a permission, and the difference is the whole design. `sealed` is a field on the mode record beside the other rules that decide what a room is, true for Ocean Gambit and false for Classic, so the lobby's card, the create form's phrase field, the guide's last page and the gate at the create route all read one flag instead of each deciding for itself whether a voyage is locked. The phrase that opens a sealed harbor is deliberately public, and that is the design rather than a leak, because a secret the table shares is a rumor and a secret the server keeps is an administrative check at the top of every evening. It is seeded in the world three ways on paper, and two of them are built. The harbor's own line is written into the voyage log of the captain whose tenth completed voyage it was, and on no other voyage. The How to Play guide keeps the sentence on its last step, which is the page a captain can actually open, and the repository's own `README.md` prints the phrase under Sealed voyages. The third seed the plan asked for, a phrase a port merchant says once, has nowhere to land, because no prose rumor surface exists in this tree; it is recorded as unbuilt rather than quietly dropped. Because the phrase is public, one table holds it for both sides (`src/lib/unlock.ts`, in the shape the mode and difficulty tables already use), and both the server and the interface read the same copy of it rather than two that could drift. What crosses the wire is the door a room was opened through, never the words that opened it: a room card names the phrase by its label, and the phrase itself stays in the table. The gate sits at the one place a room is born, the create route, and it answers three ways rather than one, because a host who brought no phrase, a host who brought the wrong words and a host who brought a phrase that opens a different voyage are owed three different sentences. A phrase is normalized before it is compared, so capitals, a trailing full stop and stray spaces all still open the door, and the raw text is never written down: the room records which door it opened, by id, in a column of its own. The line that seeds the phrase fires from the count the conclusion has just written rather than the count it read, so it lands on the voyage that crossed the threshold, and a forged finish is excluded by name, because a captain whose books were written off banks no voyage and a captain already sitting at ten would otherwise be handed the line on every fake they sailed. The phrase is a ritual rather than a fence: it is never spent, never rationed and never checked against an account, so every seat at the table sails the voyage it opens, and the first question in a harbor stays about the voyage rather than about who holds what.

One rule cuts across that order. A completed barter trade can land during any phase, because an offer surfaces in the harbor chat as well as in the exchange and a captain can take it from either. Every phase reads a captain's hold and Gold as they are rather than as they were when the phase opened, so a trade that lands partway through a phase changes what is affordable before the phase ends, and no phase holds a cached copy of those numbers to go stale.

Ocean Gambit carries two things Classic does not. Every captain is dealt a private card at departure, and it is the only thing in the game that never reaches a client it does not belong to: a hidden alignment, and for most of the table a personal goal drawn to match the commission the fleet is working on. And the whole fleet is set one public commission for the voyage, drawn before it starts, sized to the fleet it was dealt to, and shown to everyone: handing goods to it pays the Emperor's price into Gold, and any captain can hand over as much of it as their hold allows, so the board moves as the fleet moves. Both are read at the end of the voyage, and `src/lib/game/victory.ts` is where: a card wins when the thing it promised happens. An Honest card needs the fleet's commission met, and the personal goal it was dealt met with it. A Broker card needs coin this captain has taken from other captains in trade, and does not care what the commission did, which is the property the design leans on hardest, since a voyage the fleet finished well and the Broker got rich on is a voyage two sides won. A Pirate card needs the commission to fail, and needs its holder still solvent and rated at least a Qualified Trader, which is what makes the traitor run a working business rather than hide. The verdict is written to that captain's own Chronicle row and stays off every wire while there is still a leg to sail: what a captain won is as private as what they were dealt. It is broadcast once, to the whole table, in the reveal that ends the voyage, because by then there is nothing left for it to spoil. The public and private channels are described with the realtime layer below.

## The Voyage's Length, and the Table That Sails It

A mode already decided the order of the lap. It also decides how many times the lap runs, and that is the number the two records in the tree used to disagree about. The difficulty ladder is eight rounds at Fair Winds, twelve at Open Waters and sixteen at Monsoon; Ocean Gambit pins twelve on every tier, because eight is too short for deduction to develop and sixteen is too long to hold the tension. The pin is one field on the mode record, `voyageLegs` in `src/lib/game/mode.ts`, and it is 12 for Ocean Gambit and null for Classic. Null is the absence of a pin rather than a copy of the ladder, deliberately: a mode that pinned a copy of the ladder would stop following the ladder the day a tier's number moved.

The two records meet in exactly one function. `voyageRoundsFor(mode, difficulty)` returns the mode's pin when there is one and the tier's own rounds when there is not, and the difficulty ladder is read for a length nowhere else in the tree. That matters because a voyage's length is a number three things have to agree on: the lap a captain actually sails, the row the conclusion writes for them, and the ceiling the integrity pass judges their save against. Reading the tier in one place and the mode in another is exactly how those three drift, so none of the three reads a record. They read the selector, and so do the lobby's tier chips, the difficulty advisor and the guide, advice and tutorial copy, which is the whole reason a twelve leg table no longer shows a captain eight.

The number is pinned at departure, into `GameState.maxRounds` in `createInitialGameState`, which is the field every downstream reader already used: the lap's end, whether the maroon rung is still reachable, the rounds column on the chronicle row, the reputation cap the helpers earn against and the integrity ceiling. So a voyage has one length from the moment it starts rather than one length per reader. The one window that opens is a deploy in the middle of a voyage: a voyage already in flight plays the legs its own save fixed, and only a voyage starting after the redeploy gets the new number. That is written into the field's comment rather than left to be discovered. The integrity ceiling is the reader the pin mattered for most. Reading the tier there judged an honest twelve leg Gambit voyage against the eight round ceiling its tier would have run, which is a false accusation of forgery rather than a strict check, and it is why that parameter is named `roundsAllowed` rather than for what has elapsed: mid voyage it is how far the room has actually got, and at the finish line it is the voyage's own length.

The table size is the other half of the session reading, and it is on the balance page rather than in the game: the lobby fill time, one table size band at a time, measured from the moment the harbor opened to the moment the voyage set sail. The record has carried both clocks since the spine landed, so the row is a reading over data that already existed rather than a new instrument. It is charted and unjudged on purpose, because the decision behind it is a comparison between bands rather than a threshold on any one of them: a fill time that grows steeply past five seats is the evidence that six seats are not worth supporting, which no single threshold could express. Its gap line names the plan's tuning rule along with the knob that implements it, the mode's own `voyageLegs`: when a session runs long, the voyage shortens before the phases do, because the phases are where the conversation lives.

## The Deterministic Engine

The engine is client authoritative and deterministic by composition. Every captain seeds their own random stream from `roomId:userId` plus `voyageEpoch` plus `currentRound`, so two captains in the same room see identical markets and orders without the server computing anything. The server only owns the ready check voting, the host only transitions, and the genuinely shared harbor state.

What is seeded: the market card draw, the market's intel rumor pool, the order board's draw. What is not seeded: Salvage Crane refunds, Tax Evasion audits, the pirate raid roll itself, Broker's Favor order generation, Farsight free rumor selection, Boon drafting, Module drafting, the corrupt broker leak roll. This split is intentional. The deterministic stream fixes the shared economy so two captains see the same market. The personal stream keeps each captain's luck private so a lucky Salvage Crane refund on one client never desyncs another.

The engine surface is roughly 6,608 lines across 27 files in `engine/`, plus 12,623 lines across 36 files beside it carrying the shared constants, the types and the pure helpers the modules read from. Every function is either pure or takes `GameState` as the first argument and mutates it in place. There are no class instances, no singletons, no hidden state. The only side channel is the `logs: string[]` array every mutating function takes as its last argument.

## The Realtime Layer

The server is `attachRealtime`, a composition root over thirty two modules, one concern to a file: presence, checkpoint, barter, aid, loans, ventures, chat, conclusion, pulse, objective, docks, surge, rival, quickstart, status, admin, auth, gambit, audit, maroon, reveal, save, telemetry, bazaar, refits, contracts, consent, draft, voyage-log and the inbound frame limit, plus the shared types and the module that wires the rest together. The sixty two `socket.on` registrations live one concern to a file under `src/server/realtime/wiring`, twenty five leaves that nothing but the composition root imports, and the root names each of them in the order a connection takes its shape. It runs in one process alongside Next.js and attaches Socket.IO to the same HTTP server.

The trust model is deliberate. Anything inside one voyage is undefended. A captain can inflate their own Gold and mostly ruins their own afternoon. Anything that escapes into the permanent account record is server defended via the Ledger Integrity Pass. Real money math is pulled out of socket closures into pure modules (`convoy.ts`, `backing.ts`, `harborPulse.ts`) so it can be unit tested.

The private channel is the third case, and the opposite of the first: anything a captain is hiding from the table is defended by the server, all the way out to the wire, because hidden state is the only thing at this table worth attacking. A hidden alignment cannot come from the engine seed, because that seed is computed from values every client already knows, so the seed is minted by the server at departure, used once, and dropped, and what survives is the row it produced. A reload replays that row rather than drawing a new hand, and a restart deletes it, because a new voyage draws a new one. Delivery goes through `emitPrivate` in the presence module, which hands an entry to the sockets of the one captain it belongs to and to nobody else, and it is the only path by which an alignment reaches a wire: no broadcast site reads the alignment table at all, and the one reader outside this delivery path is the voyage's own end, which is where a card is turned into a verdict: during the voyage that verdict reaches the captain's private Chronicle row rather than a socket, and the single frame that ever tells a room what its captains were holding is the reveal, which is emitted once the voyage is over and every result in it has already been decided. There is exactly one wire shape that can carry an alignment, `PrivateEntry`, and nothing hidden travels by any other route.

A card carries two more things, and both are additions to the receiver's own card rather than news about the table. The first is the personal goal an Honest captain was dealt, private by the same rule the alignment is. The second is the one other Pirate, at a table that dealt two of them: the pair is meant to know each other, so that entry names a captain other than its receiver, and it is shaped so it cannot say more than the design asks, since it carries a name and an id and never a role.

That last claim is asserted rather than assumed. The smoke test keeps every frame every socket in a Gambit harbor receives and reads them back afterwards, so a role that reached the wrong captain would be caught rather than assumed absent, and it also checks that the hidden captain's own socket carried the word, so a card that never arrived at all cannot pass the sweep by being absent. The ally a Pirate pair is told about is swept the same way at a six captain table: two frames in the whole harbor may carry one, and both of them have to be the entry frames of the two captains it names.

The commission tally in `objective.ts` is the mirror image of that channel, and the contrast is the reason both exist. The objective is public by design, so its total goes to the whole room and a captain who joins late is handed it straight away. It gets the two defences a shared number needs rather than the one a secret needs: every report is a cumulative total merged by maximum rather than summed, so it cannot be counted twice however often it is sent, and what reaches the room is clamped to what the deck actually asks for at the rung the voyage pinned, so a doctored report cannot inflate anybody else's screen. The tally lives in memory, following the Harbor Pulse pattern, and it is the one thing a client is trusted to rebuild: each captain holds their own contribution in their voyage state and re-reports it on a heartbeat, so a server restart costs a few seconds rather than the board. The restart path clears it by hand, because a report of zero cannot clear a maximum merged tally.

The other end of the private channel is the reveal, and it is the one frame in the mode that carries an alignment to more than the captain holding it. It is allowed to for exactly one reason: it is emitted when the voyage is already over, from the conclusion's own handler and after the standings frame rather than with it, so there is no leg left for a leaked card to spoil, and a harbor playing Classic deals no cards and gets no frame at all. The frame is built in `src/server/realtime/conclusion.ts` out of the verdicts that handler has already decided rather than out of a second reading of them, kept in `src/server/realtime/reveal.ts` for the captain who reloads onto a finished table, and handed to a joining socket the same way the commission board and an audit finding are, which is how a browser that was not present at the conclusion reads the same hand the table saw. It carries the whole hand at once, because the reveal is the table's moment rather than a private one and a captain who reloads is owed what everyone else was told rather than a share of it; the ceremony on the screen is what paces it, one card at a time with a control that ends the wait early. A forged finish is printed as a card whose books could not be read, with the contribution and the trade profit that captain reported withheld from the frame rather than shown and doubted. The ledger beside it is reconstructed rather than replayed, because the session log a client keeps is never sent anywhere: the fleet's curve comes from the per leg trace the Chronicle rows already carry, merged leg by leg by `fleetTrace` in `src/lib/game/objectives.ts` at the highest report per good rather than at the sum, the delivered figures come from each captain's own reading of the board, which is the same reading the verdict was decided from, and the trade lines come from the `orderFills` window the Manifest Audit samples, so a trade the harbor was shown mid voyage and the same trade on the ledger are one sentence drawn through one function. A restart clears the frame in the server's map and the client's state together, since a new voyage that kept the old ledger would hand the previous table's cards to its first joiner.

The ready check protocol is the spine of the multiplayer lockstep. Every captain in the active roster must signal ready before the room advances. The active roster is every durable member minus anyone whose last reported phase is bankruptcy or endgame. This is what lets a room keep advancing once a captain goes bankrupt. It is also why a captain the harbor has just written off is still counted: a marooned captain's phase is their own business and never a terminal one, and neither is a bankrupt captain's in the mode that keeps the seat, so the leg that follows a maroon waits on the captain it was voted against like any other.

## The Phase Clock

A phase is a segment of real time, and since [B2] a table cannot be held hostage by one captain's closed laptop. Every seat of the leg carries a budget, the server owns the timer, and the expiry announces the same advance a unanimous ready set announces, so the frame a client receives never depends on which of the two ended the seat.

The budgets are data rather than constants: one number per phase on `PHASE_FACES.seconds` in `src/lib/game/phases.ts`, which is 25 seconds for Dawn, 180 for Market, 120 for Orders, 180 for Parley, 90 for Resolve and 60 for Dusk, and null for everything that is not a seat of the leg, which is the pier and the terminal phases. A phase is described in exactly one place since [B1], so a phase's length is a field on that record rather than a table beside it, and a seat that wants a different pressure is a number rather than a structure. `PHASE_CLOCK` scales every budget uniformly if an operator wants a table to move faster or slower, a number from 0 to 10 read once at boot the way the telemetry sample rate is, and 0 or the word off is a server with no clock at all: every room on manual advance, exactly as it was before the feature.

The timer lives in `src/server/realtime/checkpoint.ts` beside the seat it serves, and it is the server's own business: one timer per room, held in a map that never crosses the wire. What crosses the wire is the deadline, on `phase:ready_update` as `phaseEndsAt` and `phaseSeconds`. It does not ride the game state, and that is the design rather than an implementation detail: the engine has to stay pure, and a clock read folded into the state is a hidden input, which is exactly what the replay property depends on there being none of. A deadline is a fact about the room rather than about the voyage, so it is published beside the ready tally, on the frame that already describes the room. Both fields are null on a seat with no clock, the same shape the phase field has, a value or the absence of one, because a zero would draw as a countdown that had already run out.

The clock announces and never moves the checkpoint. A fire emits the advance, each client runs its own transition, and the reports that follow move the checkpoint and arm the next seat, which is the path a ready vote takes and the reason the single mover invariant is untouched. A fire also spends the seat's clock rather than rearming it, so a room whose clients never report is left on manual advance with no timer behind it rather than holding a deadline that has passed, until the next captain reports and the game status handler arms a fresh budget for the seat the room is standing at. An empty harbor is the one case that is not a slow table: it disarms and says nothing, because a room nobody is sitting in is not waiting on a straggler. A room that did move owes the captains still in it an explanation, so the fire says one line out loud on the harbor's own system channel before the advance goes out.

What a captain who had not acted does with that advance is the engine's `autoCommit` in `src/lib/game/engine/lifecycle.ts`: a pure function that leaves the seat by the seat's own defaults, canceling a module draft or taking up to three of the phase's own presses, and stopping the moment the phase moves or the voyage ends. The engine is still handed a phase identity and its own state and never a clock, which is what keeps a departure replayable from a save. The tuning signal rides the spine as `leg_timed_out`, carrying the tally the fire read, how many of the roster had readied and how many the roster held, with the leg stamped from the accumulator like every other loop event. A seat whose legs time out while nobody was idle is a seat that is too short.

Both halves of that image are asserted rather than described. The suite opens three harbors in one clock window, two captains idle, one captain's socket closed after departure, and one with nobody aboard at all, and reads what the clock does to each: the countdown the room was given, the advance nobody voted for, both captains being told, the line the harbor said out loud, the closed socket failing to hold the room to itself, the checkpoint that did not move, the report that arms the next seat from that seat's own budget, the abandoned harbor that closes as an emptied voyage with no leg timed out, and the tally on the record. The auto commit half cannot be held by a raw socket, because the captains in the suite have no engine behind them, so it is held in two real browsers: a captain who has not voted is shown the room's clock and it ticks with the page untouched, a leg ends with nobody's second click, both pages leaving the draft inside the budget, one through the choice it was holding and one through the defaults, and the seat that opens next is read on the port board, where a captain cannot vote, timed from that seat's own budget rather than from the clock that just ran out.

## Standing Orders

The clock above ends a seat, and [B3] is the other half of that: what happens to the captain who was not standing at it. Left alone, the seat is left by the engine's opinion rather than the captain's, which is the first boon on the board and nothing bought or sold. Standing orders are a small instruction set a captain writes once, and the clock follows it when it plays a seat nobody is standing at.

The record is `src/lib/game/standing.ts`, and it lives on the voyage state rather than on the account. Two reasons decided that, and the second is the one that matters: the Ledger Integrity Pass judges saves rather than account rows, and the plan asks that the written set survive a captain switching the feature off, which is a record the feature has to be able to leave alone. An account level set, configured once for every harbor a captain ever sails into, is the iteration a later slice would do; this is the set that plays one voyage. It holds four instructions, each naming something the engine can already do in one press: the boon to take at Dawn by id, a price for each good on the market board, whether to fill the trade board's orders the hold already covers, and whether to buy the next hull at the shipyard. Nothing in it is new capability.

Three properties keep it small enough to be safe. The vocabulary is closed, so a value the tree does not name is dropped rather than trusted: the normalizer bounds the shopping list by the number of goods the tree can price, dedupes it by good, refuses a price that is not a finite number, and answers with the default record rather than throwing, because a damaged record should cost a captain their instructions rather than their voyage. The evaluation is `workStandingOrders` in `src/lib/game/engine/standing.ts`, and its governing rule is that an order can do nothing a captain could not have done by hand: every branch calls the same exported engine function a button calls, so the purse, the hold and the hull's ceiling refuse an order exactly as they refuse a click. And the name of a boon is resolved against the board the captain was actually dealt rather than against the catalogue, so a written order can never take a card off a board its captain was not shown.

`autoCommit` does the wiring, and the switch is read once at its top: with orders off, every branch behaves exactly as [B2] shipped and the written set is left on the record untouched, which is the whole rollback the plan asks for. The default record is the switch on over an empty set, and an empty set is behaviorally the seat the clock already left, so a save written before this field existed sails exactly the voyage it was sailing. A set the captain did write is read once for the whole seat: Dawn takes the written boon when the draft dealt it and the board's own first offer when it did not, and the other three seats do their work before the departure that would have happened anyway. The record is carried across a restart and healed on load, both through the one normalizer every saved field goes through.

One detail of the market needed a second report rather than a bigger first one. A captain's own tally to the harbor pulse goes out on the advance frame, read from the state as it stood when the room moved them, and a market their orders played buys afterwards, inside the same act, so those lots cannot be in that report. The engine leaves the delta on the state instead and the phase sync hook relays it against the same round, which is safe because the server accumulates reports rather than replacing them. What the orders did is also visible in the log: each seat signs it once, after the fact, so a captain coming back to a voyage their orders played can tell the engine's lines from their own clicks.

Both halves are asserted rather than described. The suite holds the record itself against every shape of garbage, and drives the evaluation through `autoCommit` rather than beside it: an empty set reproducing [B2]'s seats exactly, the four instructions doing their work and their guards refusing what the seats would refuse, the switch off restoring all of it, a restart carrying the set and a damaged one healing to the default, and the delta summing with the captain's own report to the harbor's whole tally. The panel is held to the copy rule over the files it lives in, and the read the panel and the engine share is chosen on measured hue rather than taste, which the palette check enforces. The seat the clock runs out for a captain who wrote nothing is held in a real browser, as B2's own seats are.

## The Telemetry Spine

Every voyage that starts is one measurement, and the spine that takes it is deliberately the smallest thing that could: a record written once, when the voyage stops, and nothing at all written while it runs. The pure half is `src/lib/game/telemetry.ts`, which owns the version, the event vocabulary as a typed payload map, the family table, the voyage id and the record's own shape, plus the normalizer that reads a stored blob back or refuses it. The half that touches the world is `src/server/realtime/telemetry.ts`, which holds one voyage's events in memory while it sails and writes them as a single row at the end. The split is the one the whole realtime layer follows: a module that can be imported and checked on its own, and a thin wiring beside the sockets.

Seventeen events ship across four families. The loop family is the leg report, one line per captain per leg carrying the orders dealt, the orders filled and the distinct goods traded, which is the client's own count and is kept as a claim rather than a fact, with one line for the round that advanced, one for each leg a phase clock moved on, and the draft's own two: the path each captain sailed, filed once per captain when the deal settles with the seconds the table took to choose, and each change of papers. The market family is an offer posted, an offer filled and an offer expired, each with the goods and the Gold behind it, so the board's talk and the board's trades are two readings of one thing. The social family is a message line per message, an ask and a carry for each of the harbor's two votes, the mute a host sets and lifts, and the report a captain files. The business family is one line for a captain giving up a seat, which is a reading about a table rather than about the leg it happened in. The families the proposal names that are not instrumented are not instrumented for two different reasons. The survival layer is built and half measured: the pantry's meals and the hold's slots ride the leg report, while short rationed legs, crew losses and frostbite have a record on the voyage and no event on the spine, because nothing in this build reads them and a family declared ahead of the events that would fill it is a schema with no rows in it. The build layer waits on Epic F, so it is still prose rather than values, and the paths, which shipped with Epic D, file their two lines here rather than into a family of their own: what counts a pick rate is the path each captain sailed, and what counts a switch is the one change of papers a voyage allows, so the readings the plan names are counts over an event the record carries rather than fields waiting on a schema. Inside the families that are instrumented, the Chandler share and Bale usage wait on Epic G. Audit and Maroon accuracy is not missing for a reason that will pass: it is whether the captain the harbor voted on was guilty, and that verdict lives on the captain's own Chronicle row, where it stays.

Two rules keep the measurement from costing the game anything. Nothing awaits before it appends and nothing throws into a socket handler, so a harbor that cannot record itself still sails and a record that cannot be stored is logged rather than raised. And the leg stamped on an event is the accumulator's rather than the caller's, because a socket handler fires whenever it fires, and an event that placed itself on a leg the harbor had not reached would be a measurement of nothing. The one event a client sends is bounded rather than trusted: a report about a leg the voyage never reached is dropped, the bound allows exactly one leg of slack because the fastest captain at a table is routinely one ahead of the checkpoint, one line per captain per leg is kept with the last one winning so a client that re-reports closes the leg with later figures, and everything else in the line is the captain's reading of their own screen.

A voyage has three endings and all three leave a record. It concludes, the host wipes it, or the harbor empties out from under it, and the outcome is the record's own word for which happened, which is what makes a voyage abandoned mid leg explain where it stopped. A record outlives the room it came from on purpose: the table carries a bare indexed room id and no relation, so a room deleted out from under a voyage still has its measurement, an operator's purge is the one thing that clears a hosted harbor's rows by hand, and the smoke suite clears the rows whose room no longer exists. The one gap is the one an in-memory accumulator always has, and it is named rather than hidden: nothing is written until the voyage stops, so a process that dies mid voyage takes that voyage's record with it.

A record's captain lines are the one part of it that is a summary rather than a happening, and each line carries four things: who the captain was, whether they were still standing in the harbor when the voyage closed, whether the harbor's carried vote put them ashore, and what their own save said they took from other captains in trade. The last two landed in I2, and both are written from facts the server holds rather than reconstructed by a reader. The maroon is set at the same call that records the carried vote, so a voyage that ends by a wipe or by the harbor emptying still carries it, which is the one thing a mark derived from the events would lose. The peer ledger is read once, at the conclusion, out of the room's saves through the same reader the Broker's verdict uses, which is what keeps the number on a line and the number a rule decided on from becoming two readings of one save. That read is one query over the room's saves, taken at the moment the voyage ends, which is the only moment they exist as a set: a harbor that empties takes its saves with it when the room row goes. It hands one parse to three readers, the record's lines, the ledger integrity verdict and the chronicle, which is two reads fewer than the conclusion used to make of it. A captain no save was read for carries a zero rather than a null, which is the same reading an unreadable save gives, and a record written before the two fields existed reads the same way rather than being rewritten.

The share of voyages recorded is configuration from the first day, on the grounds that volume is the only thing about telemetry that ever needs turning down. `TELEMETRY_SAMPLE_RATE` is a fraction of one, read once at boot and validated beside every other variable, and the draw is per voyage rather than per event and deterministic off the voyage's own identity under a name of its own, so it can never share a stream with the market or the commission, and a voyage that was recorded is recorded for its whole life. The accumulator never reads a save, and that is the property to keep: the one save read the spine is part of is the conclusion's own, which hands the ledger in rather than letting a record go looking for it. No game rule reads a field of a record, so no voyage behaves differently for being measured, and the Ledger Integrity Pass has nothing new to learn, because the slice introduced no persisted save field at all: the peer ledger was already a save field the Broker's verdict read, and both new fields live inside the record rather than in columns.

## The Persistence Layer

SQLite via Prisma. The schema covers accounts, sessions, rooms, membership, per player game state, chat (room wide and one to one messages), captain legacy, captain merits, voyage chronicles, captain rivalries, convoy ventures, loans, and the private cards a Gambit harbor was dealt, plus the telemetry record a finished voyage leaves behind. The cards are the one of those no surface reads in either direction: a card is written when the table is dealt and read back only by the server that deals it, so there is no page and no report that opens the table at all. A row is one captain's card, and it holds what that captain was dealt: the alignment, and for most of the table the personal goal the alignment carried. It is written by the server at departure rather than drawn from the engine seed, because a seed the client can compute is a secret the client already has, and it is the row rather than the seed that survives, because a seed kept for the whole voyage would re-deal every card at the table the moment a late captain joined. The goal is a column for the same reason: the seed is minted, used once and dropped, so a card read back on a reload has nothing left to re-derive it from, and a goal a client could work out for itself would not be private. The one other Pirate a pair of them is told about needs no column, because it is a fact about two rows rather than about either one of them: the server reads the pair off the table at the moment it sends, so there is no second copy to fall out of step.

A finished voyage's telemetry is the one row nothing reads back during play. It is one row per recorded voyage, written once at the close, and it carries the columns a reader would query by, which are the room, the epoch, the mode, the difficulty, the seats, the outcome, the leg the voyage stopped on, the version it was written by, the sampling rate it ran under and both ends of its clock, beside the record itself as JSON. The body is a blob rather than a table of events for the reason the chronicle's trace is a blob: it is read by analysis and never by the game, so normalizing it into rows would buy joins nothing needs and pay for them on the write path that closes a voyage. The room id is a bare indexed column with no relation to `Room`, which is the one deliberate departure from the schema's habits, because a record has to outlive the room it describes: a harbor that empties out and is deleted is exactly the voyage most worth having, and a foreign key would take the measurement with the room. The reader is the normalizer in `src/lib/game/telemetry.ts`, which drops an event name it does not know and refuses a record with no voyage id rather than guessing, and it is the same defensive read pattern the save path uses. The game never reads the table back, and the one surface that does is the operator's balance dashboard, which opens it after the voyage is over through the same normalizer, beside the command line band report. Neither writes.

## The Private Information Review

[J1] The private channel is asserted by a review rather than by the sentence above, and that review is `docs/SECURITY_REVIEW.md`. Its scope is every site that builds a broadcast payload (50 statements across 16 files under `src/server/realtime`), every site that writes to the session log array, and the save path that trusts a client. It read all three end to end and fixed three findings. The detail popup relayed whatever its sender said, so any authenticated captain could push a forged snapshot at any account in the tree; the server now writes the question down when it is asked and relays an answer only for that exact pair. A manifest line was bounded in shape but not in size, so a doctored save could print a paragraph of its choosing in front of the table at the audit; a fill is now bounded in text, in kinds of good and in count, with every bound read off the game rather than picked. And a save had no size limit at all on a path the harbor pays for when a voyage closes; 64 KB is the ceiling, chosen from measurement, since five real saves run 1.5 KB to 3.5 KB. The review also names the boundaries it accepted on purpose, among them the client authoritative engine, the two room wide carriers of a secret (the reveal and the audit's sample, both deliberate and both reachable only at their own moment), and identity being bound on the socket rather than read from a payload.

Two gates came out of it, because a review that ends in a document is true exactly once. `scripts/private-scan.ts`, run as `npm run check:private`, holds seven rules that fail on a second path to anything the mode hides. `scripts/closed-test.ts`, run as `npm run check:closed-test`, is step zero of the thirty player closed test: it refuses to bless anything but a local SQLite file whose name begins with `closed-test`, prints that name beside a count of what is already inside the file, and never prints any part of the database URL. Both are run by hand rather than by the build, and the review's own limits are written beside them rather than left to be assumed: the scan reads text rather than meaning, the sweeps only read the harbors the suite sails, and the guard checks a name rather than the fact of the database's separation, which the smoke suite's interlock covers at run time.

## The Balance Reading

The dashboard is the one surface that reads the spine back, and it is three pieces rather than one so the arithmetic can be checked without a server. `src/lib/game/dashboard.ts` is the reader: records and chronicle rows in, a reading out, pure the way `src/lib/game/balance.ts` is, opening no socket, reading no clock and holding no connection. `src/server/telemetry-window.ts` is the only part that touches the database, and what it opens is the spine rather than the game: the mode's own records, newest first, capped at the launch floor of three hundred, and the chronicle rows for exactly those voyages, so a measurement and the row written beside it are read together or not at all, and every number on the page is over one window rather than each being read over whichever rows a query happened to reach. The window is one module rather than one query in one route because goal I4 adds a second reader of it, the launch gate report, and two copies of one window is how a page and a report start disagreeing about whether the mode passed: its size is taken from the launch floor rather than written beside it, so a window too small to clear is impossible to configure by accident. `src/components/portmasters/BalanceDashboard.tsx` prints what it was handed and decides nothing of its own, which is what keeps the page and the command line report from ever disagreeing about a number: both ask the same reader, and neither re-derives what the reader already said.

The window says what it is before it says anything else: how many voyages, how many captains between them, the lowest sample rate any of them ran under, and how many hit the event cap or could not be read back into a record. Those are qualifications rather than footnotes, so they sit in the header of the page beside the count a reader would otherwise take for the whole run.

The front page is the plan's front page number, the Barge revenue share, and it is not a tile built for the occasion: it is the same object as the seat panel's first reading, handed to both places by the reader, so the page cannot show two versions of the number it is named for. The number is read rather than waited on, and it is read from the leg reports: the vendor's Gold over the whole window's food spending, taken at each captain's greatest leg rather than by adding their legs up and over the voyages whose record carries the pair rather than over every voyage in the window, because a report filed before this feature is a voyage the reading was not measuring. The plan's other number, the share of lobbies that sailed without the vendor, is the seat panel's second row, read over the lobbies rather than over the captains and judged against the band the plan names, above seventy percent. Neither one is one of the sixteen gates, and that is deliberate rather than unfinished: a gate is a handle the verdict counts, and these two are readings a balance pass watches move against each other. Beside it sit the three questions the epic's evaluation asks, each with a state and one sentence, which is the minute that evaluation is asking for. The panels below carry every gate the launch goal names, each with the gate it is judged against printed beside it, so no reader has to hold a target in their head to know whether a number is good.

The rule for a gate the page cannot read is that it says so and says what it waits on. Several of the launch gates belong to systems this tree has not built, so a reading with no source prints as not measurable rather than as a zero, carrying the plan's own band as its target where the plan wrote one, and the panel's gap beside it names what the reading waits on, because a dashboard that prints 0.0% for something nobody measured is the instrument the plan warns about. A rate over an empty band prints as unplayed for the same reason, and a measurement the plan set no threshold on prints as measured but unjudged rather than being given a band nobody wrote. The verdicts are the win rate reader's own, so a cell cannot be in band on this page and out of band in the report, and a rate is printed by one formatter that lives beside the reader rather than in either place that shows one, since the report and the page print the same cells.

Nothing on the page can write. The reader has no way to, the route only queries, and the page fetches over REST once on arrival and once per Refresh rather than subscribing to anything, which is the plan's rollback made structural rather than promised. The reading is about the mode rather than about who sailed it: the window counts the captains its voyages carried and never names one, and the smoke suite asserts the absence rather than trusting it.

A participant's contribution to the fleet commission needs no table of its own. What that captain has handed over, and the per round trace of where the harbor's total stood while they watched it, ride inside the existing per voyage game state blob beside the price history. What a finished voyage leaves behind is its Chronicle row, which now carries the mode, the objective the harbor was working on, whether the last board its captain saw had met it, and the trace itself. Those trace timestamps are the point: the mode's evaluation is about how much the fleet talked while the objective was close to met, and no round boundary is recorded anywhere else, so without them the conversations could not be attributed to the legs they happened in. A Chronicle row is per captain and carries what that one client last saw, so a later analysis takes the room's maximum rather than trusting any single row.

The row also carries the two columns the win rules need: the alignment the captain sailed under and whether that card won. Both are private, because a Chronicle row is read only by the captain it belongs to, and the row is where the epic's own evaluation reads them back: every win rate band in the plan is a query over alignment and won on rows from finished voyages, and the honest side's band, which nothing could measure before, is one of them. The Broker's side of that ledger is a durable captain field, `peerTradeProfit`, because the plan is blunt that this distinction has to be instrumented from leg one rather than reconstructed later. It is accumulated at the two barter settlement paths, and only there: a port sale, a wage, a tax, a fine, a loan and a convoy payout all move Gold without ever reaching them, which is exactly the distinction the role is made of. Escrow is not counted either, since posting an offer and having it refunded is not a trade, and the two sides of one settled trade are exact mirrors of each other, which the smoke suite asserts rather than assumes. It is normalized on load beside the other voyage fields, because a save written before it existed carries no tally and the first trade added to undefined would read as an impossible save. It is registered with the Ledger Integrity Pass for the same reason money and score are, with a ceiling of its own: what it counts is cumulative volume rather than a balance, so the bound is the whole harbor's income for a round, times the headroom of the largest table the mode is authored for, rather than one captain's income.

The manifest the audit samples is the other field the mode persists for a reader rather than for the engine. Every completed order's fulfillment is pushed onto `state.orderFills` at the one path that resolves an order, and the list is capped at the five lines the sample reads, both at the push and again on load, so a save cannot grow the record past the window that has a reason to exist and a record that outlived it cannot be read by a later feature that was never designed for it. It is written as an allow list rather than filtered down to one: a fill is the port, the goods in it, the Gold the order paid and the leg it happened in, and the alignment, the hold, the Gold on hand and the card were never written into it, so there is no version of the reveal that could leak one. It is normalized on load beside the other voyage fields, because an old save has no manifest and the engine writes it unconditionally. The Ledger Integrity Pass judges it by the length of the list rather than by any number in it, against the widest order board a round can deal, because the manifest is the one record in the mode a whole table reads and believes, and a save that could stuff it would be a save that could lie to a table at once.

The Ledger Integrity Pass guards the one save endpoint that trusts a client completely. It judges an incoming save against an absolute ceiling keyed to how far the room's own voyage has actually got, derived from the live game data rather than written down, and it never rejects mid voyage. A captain mid game must never lose it to a mistaken guard. The consequence lands at voyage end. An impossible flagged captain still finishes the voyage and appears in the standings, but banks no Renown XP, no Merits, and no Sea Master crown.

## The Launch Verdict

The dashboard reads the launch gates and the verdict decides with them, and the two are separate modules on purpose. `src/lib/game/dashboard.ts` names the plan's sixteen gates in the plan's order, and every reading on the page carries the id of the gate it answers, so a gate is a handle the code can count rather than a label somebody has to match by eye: a gate the page loses is an id that stopped appearing, and a gate renamed in one place fails the build rather than quietly leaving the ship decision short by one. A role's win rate is one gate read at three table sizes, so the three cells of a role carry that role's id and are read together rather than counted as three gates.

`src/lib/game/gates.ts` is the decision, and it is a reduction over a reading rather than a second reading of the window: it takes what the dashboard returned, opens no database, reads no clock and knows nothing about where the window came from, so the page, the command line and any later surface ask one implementation and cannot disagree about whether the mode passed. The answer has three states rather than two, because "we cannot say yet" is not the same finding as "something is out of band": clear, held, and unjudged. Clear costs the whole of what the plan asks for, which is all sixteen gates inside their bands over at least three hundred recorded voyages, at a sample rate of one and with nothing unread or truncated. Every way of falling short is its own sentence in the gaps rather than a count, because a gate with no source, a gate no voyage has exercised, a run a hundred voyages short and a window recorded at half rate each ask for different work. The answer and the gap list are read off one ordered list of reasons, so the first thing an operator reads is the first thing they would work on and the two cannot name different reasons. A gate the page does not carry at all is built as an unmeasured gate rather than skipped, so the decision is taken over all sixteen or withheld. The plan's tie break, that the Free Captain pick rate wins when two gates conflict, is data in that module rather than a sentence only a document carries: it is applied when the priority gate is the one out of band, and the report prints it on every run rather than only when it is needed.

`scripts/gates.ts`, run as `npm run report:gates`, prints the sixteen gates against the window, one row each with the reading that decided the gate, and the ship decision under them. Its exit code is the verdict rather than an accident: zero clear, one held, two no verdict yet, three the report could not be read, which is what lets the command stand in a release gate instead of being read by eye. The page carries the same verdict in a strip above the front page, computed from the reading it already fetched rather than fetched again, so the launch state costs no second query and cannot be read over a different window than the panels under it. Every word on that strip is written by the decision module, which is what keeps the strip and the report from counting a gate two ways.

What none of this produces is the run. Three hundred recorded voyages are played by the operator rather than by the tree, so until they are played the verdict reads unjudged and says what it is short of, in the same words on the page and in the report.

## The Paths

`src/lib/game/paths.ts` is one record per path and the single source of truth for what a path is: a captain's identity at a table, built around one verb and one ability that acts on another captain, carrying the crest a locked order card wears, the sentence that card prints, the goods the ability touches, the goods whose manifest orders lock to the path, the multiplier its hold carries and the Renown ceiling it banks to. It is the difficulty ladder's own shape, and it exists for the ladder's own reason: the board, the engine and the interface all have to agree about what a path is, and the way they agree is that exactly one of them writes it down. The one structural decision worth naming is that the id type is derived from the record (`PathId` is `keyof typeof PATHS`, and `PATH_IDS` is read off the record's keys), so there is deliberately no enum, no second list of ids and no hand written order array: a sixth path is one entry in one file, which is the property the plan's evaluation was written to test and which the cycle that built this proved by adding one to a copy of the tree and building the whole of it with nothing else edited. The record reads no clock, no database, no socket and no environment value, so retuning a path is an edit to one file rather than a deploy.

The two numbers the design leaves to the reader are read from the things they were always about rather than typed in as levels. Convoy's hold is its cannon slots subtracted from the hold, because the guns that make its contract worth buying are cargo it cannot carry, and the Quartermaster's is the hold plus half again; both knobs live in `constants/paths.ts` beside the hold they retune, and neither is applied anywhere a captain's cargo is counted, because both land at `cargoCapacity` in the larder, which is the hold's single capacity read, so a path's modifier and a hungry crew's quarter can never become two answers to what fits. The ceilings come off `RENOWN_TITLES`: the top rung for the Quartermaster and the rung beneath it for the four beside it, so a title added above the top moves every ceiling with it. The goods are read off the tables the features live in, the garments and the commodities and the three foods, each by its own table's keys, so a fourth garment reaches a Loom captain's pool without an edit in the record.

Three readers are the whole of the public surface. `normalizePath` answers a saved value as one of the paths this build has or as null, and null rather than a default is deliberate: the house shape for an unknown value falls back (normalizeMode, normalizeDifficulty, normalizeRole) and this one must not, because a captain may legitimately hold no path at all and a fallback would be an identity nobody chose. Its membership test is `Object.hasOwn` rather than `in`, so the one string the prototype chain would have answered for reads as no path like any other junk. `pathConfig` is the record lookup every other module goes through, and `lockingPathFor` is D2's reader: a good's locking path is looked up in the pools rather than written onto a card, so a retuned pool cannot desynchronize from the label it explains, and the two paths whose ability is an action rather than an errand carry no pool, since selling a contract and borrowing an order are not errands the board can post.

The naming change landed with the module and it is recorded where the words were rather than only here. A captain's identity in this game is a path, and a House is what it always was, the account level colour a captain sails under; faction existed in exactly one place in this tree, a comment in `palette.css` naming the Great Houses' colours, and it was swept in the same pass. The word Variable is retired rather than renamed, and the reason is that a word a captain can spend on the thing somebody is hiding turns the first question at every table into an accusation: it existed as this tree's own word for a hidden card, in `gambit.ts`, in a schema comment and in the suite's labels, no screen ever printed it, and both retired words are now refused as path ids by the reader the smoke suite checks. One collision was recorded here rather than resolved, because resolving it was not this slice's to do: the word Convoy already names a pooled contribution on the harbor's venture board, and the path shares it. D3 resolved it by moving the venture's player facing label from Convoy Ventures to Ventures, so the path keeps the name its own content and its draft are written in and the one word a captain meets on the boards names one thing again. The venture's code keeps its historical identifiers, since a persisted model's name is a migration rather than a copy change; the reasoning sits with the words it moved, in `src/lib/game/convoy.ts` and `src/lib/game/paths.ts`. What this record does not do is deal itself: a path reaches a captain through the draft below, which is room state rather than content, and a table that never drafts reads pathless rather than defaulted, which is the state its own reader is built to answer honestly.

## The Pathbound Order Board

The trade manifest carries the tier's own draw and three cards the paths own, and the two halves come from different places on purpose. `startOrders` in `src/lib/game/engine/orders.ts` fills the six a charter schedules first, the count read from the difficulty ladder the port board also reads, and then appends `PATH_ORDER_SLOTS` cards drawn on a seeded stream of their own rather than extending the round's stream. That separation is the backward compatibility guarantee stated as a mechanism: the six are identical with the feature on or off, which the suite checks card for card and reward for reward, so a table playing the base game is dealt the board it was always dealt. The card carries one field, `isPathOrder`, a marker and nothing more. Which path a card waits on is derived at read time, through `pathOrderOf` asking `lockingPathFor` for the good the card demands, so the label is computed from the path record rather than stored on the card and a retuned pool moves the labels with it. The generator that deals them, `genPathOrder` in `./engine/market`, filters the trade pools to goods some path claims and then delegates to the raw or the product generator by the good's own kind, so a pathbound card asks what the manifest pays for that good and never at a discount.

The lock rule has one home and one reading per surface. `lockedBehind(state, order)` answers the path a card waits on for this captain, null where the card is theirs or where they hold the path it wants, and `canFillOrder` is that together with the hold's own shortfall: one guard, asked by the board's buttons, by `completeOrder`, by the standing orders and by the ActionSuggester, which is why the second copy of the rule a standing order would otherwise need does not exist. `completeOrder` asks the lock before it asks the hold, so a locked card whose goods are aboard is refused as somebody else's rather than as a short inventory, and both the sentence on the card and the line in the log come from `pathLockLine`, held to one carrier in the tree by the suite. The switch, `pathOrdersOn` read through the shared `flagOn` policy, is read at both ends, at the draw and at the lock, which is the plan's rollback clause kept whole: a board dealt while the feature was on plays as six ordinary orders once it is off rather than leaving three cards grey forever, and the leg report's dealt count reads through `openOrderCount` so a locked card is not filed as an order its captain failed to fill. Measured on the isolated copy: dealing a board costs 2.8 microseconds more with the feature on, 4.84 against 2.06 per deal, a full pass of the readers over the nine cards costs about 0.6 microseconds, and the drawn board is on screen 155 to 185 milliseconds after the ready click. The worst main thread task in the same lap ran 63 to 94 milliseconds across four runs, inside the 55 to 67 millisecond range the lap produces in phases this feature does not touch, so the three cards do not move the frame budget in anything this environment can resolve.

## The Escort Contract

`src/lib/game/engine/contracts.ts` is everything an agreement means on a captain's own machine, and the board itself is room state in `src/server/realtime/contracts.ts`, because an agreement between two captains is an object no single client can be the authority over. It is the split the barter board runs on and for the same reason: the server brokers the agreement and relays the claim, and a purse is only ever touched by its owner. A contract is offered by a seller, agreed when a buyer accepts, and claimed by the seller when the covered captain reports the raid it absorbed. The fee moves at the moment the two captains agree rather than when a raid lands, which is the plan's "contracts pay first", so the seller is paid for the leg whatever the sea does and eats a claim only when one comes. What the guns beat off is one number, `CONVOY_RAID_COVERAGE` in `constants/paths.ts`, read by the subtraction the seller's side applies and by the two panels that quote the odds, so a captain reading their chances and the engine settling a claim are reading the same figure rather than two that happen to agree. The path is a reading of the record rather than a second name for it, the way D2 reads a lock off the good a card demands: `ESCORT_SELLER_PATH` names the path once, and the ability question is the only place the id is spent.

The board is personalized per connected socket rather than broadcast whole, unlike every other room wide broadcast in this tree, and that is the point rather than an inconsistency: a direct offer belongs to two captains and a claimed contract's raid figure belongs to its seller, so two captains in one room can legitimately see two different boards. The policy deciding which rows a captain may read lives in `visibleContracts` beside the rest of the board's rules rather than in the server, and all three places that send a board go through one payload function, so a broadcast, the answer to a state request and the hydration a joining socket is handed cannot disagree about a field. The board is transient by the plan's own instruction, and it is worth saying what that leaves on a save: nothing here is persisted, and what a save carries is the tally of what already settled and the ledger of movements already applied, both of which are records of the past rather than live agreements.

Two trust boundaries are stated in the board module because they are decisions rather than omissions, and both are inherited from the barter board rather than invented here. The server does not know who holds the Convoy path and does not ask, because a path lives in a captain's own save beside their hold and their purse and the server has never read one. And the server prices no claim and clamps none either, since what a raid would have taken is the covered captain's own report and what the guns ate is worked out on the seller's client against the seller's own purse, so there are no numbers on the server that a report could be checked against. What the server does enforce is the claim's ordering: one claim per contract, only against a contract that was agreed, only from the captain whose name is on it, and only in the leg it was sold for. The bound that matters is the one the seller accepted when they sold, which is that the worst a claim can cost them is the hold they were standing in.

The gaps the plan leaves for betrayal are held open deliberately, and one of them is narrowed rather than closed. A captain who leaves the harbor takes their contracts with them, both the offers nobody had taken and the agreements already struck, because the cover is read off this board and a board still carrying a departed seller's contract would leave the buyer protected by guns that are no longer in the harbor. That costs the buyer the protection they paid for, which is the plainest of the gaps. What the rule deliberately does not key on is the buyer's own departure, because a contract the seller still owes on is the seller's to keep. The switch, `NEXT_PUBLIC_ESCORT_CONTRACTS` read through the shared `flagOn` policy, is D3's rollback: with it off no contract can be posted, no board is drawn and the raid roll reads no cover, so a voyage plays exactly the raid it played before the feature existed. What is deliberately not here is the reading the plan asks to watch, contracts sold per leg per Convoy captain and the ratio of fees collected to losses absorbed, and the reason is not a gap in the record but an empty market: a Convoy captain only exists once a fleet deals its paths, which the draft below now does, so the panel has its seller and waits on tables with strangers at them rather than on anything missing from the tree.

## The Refit Bench

The refit is the same transaction the escort contract is, which is the design's own instruction rather than a coincidence: one captain offers a service at a price, another accepts it, and each side then moves its own purse. `src/lib/game/engine/consent.ts` is that shape and names neither an escort nor a garment, so the rules every agreement shares are written once. What a fee is (`consentFeeFor`, whole Gold between `CONSENT_FEE_MIN` and `CONSENT_FEE_MAX`, a fraction floored and a value outside the bounds refused rather than clamped, because quietly charging a price somebody did not name is the one behaviour a market cannot have). Which rows a captain may read (`visibleConsent`, which hides an offer addressed to somebody else). When an offer stops being one (`expireConsent`, which reads the phase and the leg off the row rather than comparing against a constant). And the ledger that keeps a reload from moving the same Gold twice (`movementApplied` and `markMovement`, keyed on the agreement and the term). What is deliberately not shared is the four things the two kinds differ on: who may sell, what is being traded, what an agreement does to a state, and which side is bounded to one agreement a leg. `src/lib/game/engine/refits.ts` is D4's answer to all four, and `src/server/realtime/refits.ts` binds the shared board factory to this bench's event and payload, `refit:update` carrying `{roomId, refits}` where the market carries `{roomId, contracts}`, which is a wire field name and therefore a contract with every client already connected. The client side is split the same way, one relay hook for both kinds in `src/lib/use-consent-board.ts` and one bench hook for what only the refit can say in `src/lib/use-refit-contracts.ts`. There is no claim on this board, and the absence is the interesting half: a refit settles the moment it is agreed, because both halves of it are known at that moment, while the escort's claim exists because what a raid would have taken is a number that does not exist until Resolve.

What the bench sells is a number and the number is the design. A refit puts `REFIT_POINTS` back on one garment in a single leg, and the harbor tailors, who are what every other captain can do alone, put `MEND_POINTS` back for `MEND_GOLD_PER_POINT` Gold once a leg. So the trade has something to be cheaper than, which is what makes "faster and cheaper than they could manage alone" a market rather than a sentence. `refitRoomFor` reads `min(REFIT_POINTS, garmentRoom(state, good))`, so the points a row quotes and the points the customer's warmth moves by are one number rather than two that agree, and a garment with nothing left to put right hides the accept before the click instead of refusing it after one. The path's own supply is the pile, and the pile is drawn per captain from the voyage's own weather: `ragsAtPort` is zero on a warm leg and otherwise one to `RAGS_AT_PORT_COLD` seeded on the voyage, the mode, the difficulty and the leg, and `ragsLeftAtPort` subtracts what this leg already took through a stamp, so a count from an earlier leg is not spent against this one. It is a share rather than a shelf, and that is a rule about desynchronization: a stock two clients can empty is a number neither can see the other's spending of, while a pile each captain reads on their own machine and takes their own share of is the number both can already read. A rag costs `RAG_SCRAP_VALUE`, one at a time, takes a slot of the hold through the one capacity reader rather than a second count of its own, and `REWEAVE_RAGS` of them go back on the loom as one `REWEAVE_GOOD`, which is the crafting chain's own ratio read off the recipe table rather than typed beside it.

Settlement is one function for both sides, the shape the escort's own takes, because the two cases differ in who they are and in which direction the Gold moves and agree on everything else. `applyRefitSide` moves the fee first so the log reads in the order the captain lived it, caps what the buyer pays at the purse they actually have rather than writing a negative hold, puts the points back on the customer's own garment on the customer's own machine, and marks the movement in the ledger so a reload between the agreement and the broadcast that carries it cannot charge the same fee twice. What the server enforces is the ordering rather than the size: an offer is posted in the Market and dies with it, a refit is agreed at a port in the Market phase, one seller takes on one refit a leg, one offer stands per captain named, a row belongs to the leg it was posted in, and an agreed refit cannot be withdrawn. Every one of those is a sentence a captain is owed rather than a silent drop, and the panel carries the server's own refusals so a board that lags a checkpoint shows the reason before a click instead of after it. The two trust boundaries are the board's rather than this bench's and are worth restating because a second kind now rides them: the server does not know who holds the Loom path and does not ask, since a path lives in a captain's own save, and the server prices no term and clamps none, since what a garment needs and what a purse holds are both worked out on the clients that own them.

The rollback is `NEXT_PUBLIC_REFITS`, read through `refitsOn` in `src/lib/game/engine/refits.ts`, and it is worth saying that it is not a bare switch: it stands on `NEXT_PUBLIC_GARMENTS` as well, because a table with no clothes layer has nothing to put right and a bench opened inside one would be selling work on a wardrobe that does not exist. With it off no offer can be posted, no board is drawn, no pile is drawn, and the harbor tailors take no work, so a voyage plays exactly the voyage it played before this feature existed. Nothing about a voyage is stored differently either way, which is the plan's own instruction kept rather than a happy accident: no agreement is durable, a save carries no refit, and what it does carry is the tally of what already settled and the ledger of movements already applied, both records of the past. `normalizeRefitState` heals that tally at the load site the way every field this build added is healed, eight counters floored and two stamps healed to zero, so a save written before this feature reads back as a captain who has never mended anything rather than as one whose harbor worked on them on leg one. What the plan asks to watch is income variance by leg type, that a Loom captain is the poorest at the table on warm legs and among the richest on cold ones, and that reading needs tables with strangers at them rather than a suite. What the suite pins is the mechanism the reading would read: `scripts/smoke/` holds the bench in fifty seven checks, thirty four of them pure and twenty two over the wire with three captains in a real harbor, from the fee's bounds and the pile's weather through the visibility rule that keeps an addressed offer off a third captain's board, the leg sweep that empties the bench, and the dash rule over every file this feature's copy lives in.

## The Bazaar Rumor

`src/lib/game/engine/bazaar.ts` is the whole rule and reads no socket, no database and no clock; `src/server/realtime/bazaar.ts` holds the room's rows, because who spoke in this harbor is a fact no single client can be the authority over; `src/lib/use-bazaar-rumors.ts` is the client's own read of the wire; and `src/components/portmasters/game/BazaarRumors.tsx` is the desk, drawn at the Parley table under the protection market. It is the split the escort market and the refit bench already run on, and it is the third market this tree holds in room state. Nothing in the slice is called a rumor, which is worth a line because the name was taken: the Broker's Rumor is an intel purchase this build already ships, so the feature takes the design's own distinguishing word and the module, the server board, the relay, the panel, the switch and the three socket frames all carry Bazaar rather than a second Rumor in the tree.

The visibility rule is a type and one function rather than a rule each screen remembers. A row is `BazaarRumor`, which carries the direction; the shape every client is handed is `PublicRumor`, which is that row with a direction that may be null; and `publicRumors` is the one function that turns the first into the second, which every board that travels goes through: the broadcast a publish makes, the answer to a state request, and the hydration a joining socket is handed. A row stands while the market it moves has not been drawn yet, read as `row.round >= round`, and a standing row that is not the reader's own is handed over with the direction stripped and nothing else changed. Two captains in one room therefore read two different boards in the same leg, which is the design rather than a leak in it, and the reveal rides the same advance frame that carries the lean: the frame is delivered first and the personalized board second, so a captain is told the reason for a price move on the same leg the port prices it rather than a leg later.

The arithmetic is folded into the market rather than applied to it afterwards, which is the shape the Harbor Pulse and the Harbormaster's hand already had. One expression in `genResourceCard` prices a card against the pulse, the shift and the rumor together, so the product is rounded once rather than three times, and the suite carries a card off a real leg's draw where rounding the hands one at a time would land somewhere else, so the claim is a check rather than an agreement. A rumor is read by the very next market and no other, written as `row.round + 1 === round` from the other end of the expression the Harbormaster's call is read by. Two captains naming one good are summed and then clamped to `RUMOR_SHIFT_FRACTION`, so the loudest the bazaar can get is the loudest one call can get, and two pulling against each other cancel rather than averaging into a move nobody called. A good whose net lean is nothing is dropped rather than carried as a zero, which is the rule `normalizeRumorLean` applies on the way back in, so a market nobody leaned and one leaned back to level are one record rather than two spellings of it, and a save that has been round tripped is the record it started as. On the way in from a save the lean is clamped to the same band and everything that is not a finite number is dropped, which is the defensive half of the design's own guard on how swingy prices may become: a save edited to lean a good by half is read as a rumor's worth and no more.

What the server is authoritative about is worth stating beside what it deliberately is not, because this slice draws the line in a different place from the two markets above it. It does not know who holds the Aroma path and does not ask, which is the same line the barter board and both consent boards draw, since a path lives in a captain's own save and the server has never read one. It is authoritative about the three things a row has to be true about for the feature to work at all. A rumor is spoken at the Parley, so the table hears it and the market it moves is the next one. It names a good the coming market actually trades, checked through `rumorGoodAllowed` against the market's own list read one leg ahead rather than against the catalogue, which is what lets a captain speak about a cargo in the leg before the harbor stocks it. And it respects the cooldown, which is measured off the room's own rows rather than off a counter a client could carry, because the wait between two of one captain's rumors is a fact about this room's voyage and the one rule here a doctored client could otherwise simply not obey. The desk draws all three readings before the click, so a refusal is read rather than received, and none of those readings makes the row: the server's own check is what puts a rumor on the board, and the id is minted by the board from the publisher and the leg so that "one captain, one rumor, one leg" holds where the rows are written rather than where they are asked for.

Departure is the one place this board disagrees with the two consent boards, and the disagreement is the feature. A departing captain's rows stay: they were published in the open, the harbor heard them, and the market they move prices them whether or not the captain who said it is still ashore, so dropping the row would reprice every good for the captains still sailing, which is a settlement applied halfway dressed as a cleanup. The rows go when the voyage does, and nothing here is durable at all, so a save carries no row. What a save carries is the lean the market priced against, which is the market's own copy of the fact.

The rollback is `NEXT_PUBLIC_BAZAAR`, read through the shared `flagOn` policy so that no flag in this tree can accept a value another refuses, and it is read at both ends of the draw rather than in the middle of it: the server sends no lean at all when the feature is off, and the market folds none in when it prices a card. Reading it inside `rumorLean` as well would leave a function that can only answer an empty object, which is a second place the switch is written and so a second place it can disagree with the first. With it off no rumor can be published, no board is drawn and no price moves, so a voyage plays exactly the voyage it played before this feature existed. Nothing is stored differently either way, and the honest version of that sentence carries one clause: the lean is a field every save carries whether or not a rule reads it, the way the Larder is, so the shape a voyage is saved in is the same in both builds, and `normalizeRumorLean` at the load site means a save written before this feature reads back as a market nobody leaned rather than as a captain who spoke on leg one.

Measured on the isolated copy, the rule costs nothing a frame can notice and the arithmetic is free. A board of eight rows, which is one row per captain per leg and larger than most voyages produce, is read for its lean in 0.0005 milliseconds, and personalizing it for eight readers costs 0.0025. A board a thousand times that size, eight thousand rows, still takes 0.019 milliseconds to lean and 0.44 to personalize, so the read the server does on every publish and every advance is linear in a number that cannot get large. Pricing a whole port costs about six microseconds, which is the draw rather than the lean: measured in both orders on purpose, the four cases of no lean, one rumor, two rumors and every nameable good land between 5.6 and 6.9 microseconds and the ordering flips between runs, while the case measured first in a cold process reads 13.2 and is the JIT tiering up rather than anything the feature added. No database write happens anywhere in this feature, no schema changed, no dependency was added, and the only per frame work is one broadcast on a publish and a constant sized object on the advance frame.

What the design asks to watch is false positives, how often a price move is cited as evidence against a captain in the hidden role mode and whether honest rumors account for a real share of those moments, and that reading needs tables with strangers at them rather than a suite. What the suite pins is the mechanism that reading would read: forty three checks in `scripts/smoke/`, twenty four of them pure and eighteen over the wire with three captains in a real harbor and one more filed with the log surfaces where this feature's own line is read, from the cooldown at both ends and the sentence a waiting captain is shown, through the visibility rule and its one leg window, the goods the coming market trades read one leg ahead, the lean summed and clamped and dropped when it nets to nothing, a market drawn twice from one seed with the same cards and different prices, and the one rounding that makes three hands one price. Another twenty five run in a browser, where the same room and the same row are read off two captains' screens in the same leg: one page carries the direction clause and the other carries the sentence saying the direction is not theirs yet, which is the one claim no socket check can make.

## The Free Captain's Borrow

`src/lib/game/engine/opportunist.ts` is the whole of the rule, and it is a rule about orders rather than a second order board: it reads a state, a locked path and a face value, returns a permission, a payout and three sentences, and knows nothing about where the board was drawn or how a fill is settled. That is why the module imports no part of the order engine and why the order board imports it, rather than the two reaching into each other. The pieces that are not the rule sit where the rest of their kind already sits: the allowance and the penalty are `OPPORTUNIST_USES` and `OPPORTUNIST_PENALTY` in `constants/paths.ts` beside `PATH_ORDER_SLOTS`, the counter is one integer on the save beside `brokersFavorUsed`, and the one wire field a voyage reports rides the path orders switch across the four layers every telemetry field in this build crosses. Nothing new is a switch, and that is the plan's own rollback clause rather than a shortcut: the feature's off state is D2's, because a borrow is a way through a lock and a build with no locks has nothing to borrow past.

The rule is one question asked twice. `opportunistMayBorrow` answers whether this captain may reach through this lock at all, and it is the switch, then the path, then whether the card is locked at all, then the allowance, in that order, so the cheapest reading is the one that refuses a build with the feature off and the dearest is only paid on a card that is genuinely somebody else's. The arithmetic is one expression: `opportunistPayout` takes the face value, floors it, subtracts the penalty rounded to whole Gold, and floors again at nothing, so a reward of twenty five pays fifteen and the number is never a fraction, never negative and never above the face. The penalty lands at the one line in `completeOrder` that names a reward, `let reward = order.reward;`, so the fill's freight, its VAT and its purse all read a single number afterwards rather than three that were each adjusted, which is the same one-expression shape the bazaar's price and the harbor's hand already have. The card's own figures follow: the board prices every figure it prints off the reward basis, so a locked card held by a Free Captain quotes the payout as its Reward and the net below it as that payout less its freight and its VAT, which the browser check reads off the card and holds against the purse's own movement rather than against a second guess. A borrow is asked for through a default parameter, `borrow = false`, so the two callers that fill orders without one, the standing orders and the ActionSuggester, cannot spend an allowance by omission and a fourth caller cannot either without naming it.

The allowance is spent by the fill and reset by the voyage rather than by the round. It lives on the save as `opportunistBorrows`, a count of what has been taken rather than what is left, so the field only ever climbs inside a voyage and the reader that subtracts it, `opportunistBorrowsLeft`, is the one place the limit is applied. What that buys is the sentence a captain can be handed twice without either reading being wrong: the card prints how much of the allowance is left before the press and the same count after it, and the plan's own line about a reader finding the once-per-voyage limits together is kept by where the counter was put rather than by a comment asking for it. `normalizeOpportunistBorrows` heals the field at the load site the way every field this build added is healed, so a save written before this feature reads back as a captain who has borrowed nothing rather than as one who spent the allowance on leg one.

There is one trust boundary here and it is the same line every client-authoritative fill in this build draws. The server does not know whether a borrow happened, does not price one and clamps none, because the purse it would be judged against is the captain's own save and the server has never read one. ## The Path Draft

`src/lib/game/draft.ts` is the rule and holds no state, no clock and no socket: the deck's composition, the shuffle, the hands, the keep, the pass, the window and the fee. `src/lib/game/engine/draft.ts` is the two writes a path leaves in a captain's own save and the one guard that refuses the second of them. `src/server/realtime/draft.ts` is the room's half, and it is a room half rather than a phase of the lap for the plan's own reason: a hand of cards is private information and the client cannot be trusted to deal it, so the deck never crosses the wire and what a captain is sent is their own hand and a count of the table still choosing (`DraftView`), which is the whole of what a wait is made of and is a number rather than a card. The client is one hook and one panel, `src/lib/use-path-draft.ts` and `PathDraft.tsx`, and the panel draws nothing at all with the switch off, so the screen a rolled back build shows is the screen this tree showed before the feature existed. The split is the one D6's opportunist set, moved out one layer: the rule is pure so the suite can hold the whole deal without a server, the engine is the only thing that touches a save, and the room owns everything that is about a table rather than a captain.

The draft runs on its own clock rather than on the lap's, which is why it is not a checkpoint: three steps of `DRAFT_STEP_SECONDS`, fifteen seconds each and forty five in all, which is the plan's target stated as the arithmetic that reaches it. A step closes the instant the last seat answers rather than waiting the clock out, so the forty five seconds is a ceiling and not a pace, and a table of two is finished in the time it takes to press three buttons. A captain who lets the clock run out keeps the first card of their own hand (`DRAFT_AUTO_PICK`), which is a card they were dealt rather than one the server liked, and a fire for a deadline the draft has already moved past is dropped, so a timer that outlived its step cannot resolve the step that replaced it. The deck is a table of weights rather than a list of cards, because the plan's iteration clause is to tune the composition separately from the pass rules and a table is what keeps that tuning one edit: `DRAFT_DECK_WEIGHTS` is typed as a record of every path, so a sixth path is a compile error at this table rather than a path that silently never appears in a draft, the composition is largest remainder over those weights so the counts sum to the deck size for any table, and the deck is dealt out entirely because it is sized three cards a captain. Then the Quartermaster floor, and it is taken last on purpose: the plan asks that card to be always physically present, which is a guarantee rather than a lean, so whatever the weights say the deck carries at least `DRAFT_QUARTERMASTER_MIN` of them, taken off the largest count that is not the seat's so the floor bends the composition as little as it can, and deliberately not expressed as a weight, because a knob that can be tuned to zero is not a guarantee.

The pass is `passLeft`, and its direction is the plan's: the rest of a hand goes to the next seat in the order the draft was opened with, wrapping at the table's end, so a table of one passes to itself, which is the honest shape of a solo voyage rather than a case to special case. A keep is an index rather than a card, and that is not a wire detail: a deck with a floor can hand one captain two cards of the same path, so keeping the Quartermaster does not name a card, and `keepFrom` answers null for a pick that is not in the hand, which every caller reads as a refusal rather than as a default. The switch's when is one predicate and one sentence, read by both ends of the wire because the room publishes a switch and the captain's own engine charges for one: legs three through nine are two constants and `pathSwitchWindow`, and the port is the three seats where the fleet has its books open, which is Market, Orders and Parley, the seats where a captain buys, commits to a manifest and talks to the table. What the two surfaces say is one sentence per fact rather than two that could drift, which is why `pathSwitchOpenLine` and `pathSwitchSpentLine` are exported off the engine and read by the room's handler: a room that refused a switch in words of its own would be one rule reported twice. The window's sentence asks the season before the seat, so a switch attempted in the wrong stretch of a voyage is told about the window rather than about the phase it happened to be attempted in, and the seat's sentence names the three seats by reading the same list the predicate reads (`pathSwitchPortsList`) and the same labels the seats are drawn under, so a harbor cannot name a seat it does not open. The fee is linear in the ladder with a ceiling, the shape the Renown starting gold takes and for its reason, and the guard's questions are asked in the order a captain meets them: what they hold, whether they have already changed it, whether the harbor is reading papers this leg, whether they are standing at a port, whether the destination is somewhere new, and only then whether they can pay, so that the answer you already hold that path is never a price.

The draft is the whole of the feature's transient state and it is exactly that: room memory and nothing else. There is no table, no row and no save field for a hand, a pick or a step, and a draft dies with the voyage that dealt it, which is the plan's rollback clause read as a blast radius. What a path leaves behind is two writes on the captain's own save, and both are the captain's own: `applyDraftPath` writes the card the last step left them holding, and `applyPathSwitch` pays the fee, forfeits the orders the old papers opened and stamps the leg it happened on. The stamp is both the record and the guard, so a switch cannot be recorded without being spent, and the forfeiture reads the lock through `pathOrderOf`, so cards go by the good they demand rather than by any marker written on them, which is D2's reading kept. The fee is a charge and not a ledger line: it leaves the purse and is deliberately kept out of the round's costs, because papers are bought and a switch is not the voyage's business any more than a bribe is. The publication is the design rather than the plumbing. The room publishes to the whole fleet, the log line is written for the fleet to read back with the leg on it, and the switching captain's own client applies the change off that frame rather than off its own press, so a switch the server refused is a switch that cost nobody anything. Two trust boundaries follow from that and both are stated where they are drawn: the server does not know who holds what, because a path lives in a captain's own save and this server has never read one, and the one thing the room keeps to answer once per voyage for itself is a book of which captains have spent the allowance, held in memory as a set of ids and dropped with the voyage, while the stamp in the save is the half of that sentence which survives a restart.

The rollback is `NEXT_PUBLIC_PATH_DRAFT`, read at the three places the feature can be entered: the deal on the room's start, the room's switch handler and the panel that offers the change. It is worth saying what the off state is, because it is not a second mode: nothing before this feature ever wrote `state.path`, so a build with the switch off sails the pathless voyage every earlier build sailed, and every pathbound card is locked, which is a refusal the D2 lock already answers without a single new rule. There is no second flag and no second state to roll back to, because the only way to hold a path is this deal, and a captain who changes theirs changes something the deal gave them. Nothing about a voyage is stored differently either way, and that sentence is the plainest of its kind in this tree: the draft is not stored at all, and the two fields a path leaves behind are fields every save already carried. What the design asks to watch is three readings off one event. `path_taken` is filed at the settle, one per captain, carrying the path and the seconds the table took from the deal to the last pick, measured by the server rather than reported by the client because the draft's clock is the server's; the share of drafts where a Quartermaster was taken and the pick spread per path are counts over that same event rather than fields that have to be kept in step, and the spread reads against the plan's twelve to twenty eight percent band, which the level weights put the five paths inside by construction rather than by tuning. The switch reports itself the same way, one `path_switched` event per change, which is the plan's iteration clause about watching the switch as usage.

Measured on the isolated copy, the deal is arithmetic on a table nobody is waiting for and the per render guard is free. Composing and shuffling a twelve captain deck, which is the largest table this lap seats and thirty six cards, costs 1.86 microseconds, and dealing it into twelve hands costs 0.42 more; the composition on its own is 0.30, a keep 0.018 and the whole pass of twelve hands 0.033 microseconds, so the deal that runs once at departure is not a number any frame can notice. The readers a panel or a handler calls per render are the same size: the window is 0.03 microseconds, the seat 0.01, the fee 0.05, the guard over a real save 0.18, the draft's own write 0.14 and the switch's, which pays a fee, walks a manifest and stamps a leg, 0.42. Nothing in the feature holds a socket open, and nothing in it writes a database row during play. What the suite pins is the mechanism those readings would read: forty nine checks in `scripts/smoke/` in five passes, the deal's arithmetic from the composition's counts through the floor that holds at every table size, the pass and the auto pick, the window at both ends and the fee's walk up the ladder, the two writes a kept card and a switch leave in a captain's own save with their forfeiture and their refusals, the deal played on a real harbor with two captains whose frames are read back against the rule module to the last card and every frame either socket received on any event read for a hand that is not the one that seat was dealt, and the switch published to that same fleet with its log line filed beside the voyage. Another eighteen run in a browser, where the three beats, the cards with their faces, the wait and its count, the settled identity on the chip and the switch's own surface are read off a real screen while a second captain holds cards the whole time, which is the one reading only a second seat can give.

## The Supply Barge

`src/lib/game/engine/barge.ts` is the rule and it is the whole of the feature's engine: the quay, the lot, the price, what is left of the lot, the sale and the heal. It reads the voyage's own numbers and the constants and holds nothing else, and `src/lib/game/constants/supplies.ts` carries the two numbers the plan names, `BARGE_PRICE_MULTIPLIER` at one point eight and `BARGE_RATIONS_PER_LEG` at eight, so the premium is a markup on `RATION_PRICE` rather than a second price that could drift from it, and the ceiling is the size of the lot rather than a shelf. The quay is drawn from the room's own unlocked ports at the voyage's current leg, which is the same read the goods and the market breadth take, so the vendor arrives with the charters the table is already trading on and never stands somewhere nobody can reach; the lot is a second draw on the port and the leg together, so two quays on one leg do not answer alike, and a table that met the same eight rations at every port would be looking at a rule rather than at a vendor. That is the whole of what the plan's "deterministic from port and leg" costs, and it is the harbor's rag pile read from the other end: one seed, one function, and both clients land on the same number without a frame being sent.

The vendor sells grain, and that is the plan's word read through this tree's pantry rather than a fourth thing to decide. The sale is booked at the till the way every other purchase here is booked, and the two counters move at the one line where the Gold leaves the purse: `foodSpend` is the denominator the page's share is divided by and `bargeSpend` is its numerator, written where the sale happened rather than summed later out of the logs, because a reader that added up log lines would be reading a rendering of the sale instead of the sale.

The three ceilings a press is held to are the lot, the stores and the purse, and all three are reached rather than refused, which is the same merchant the port counters are: a captain who asked for four with three left buys three. What comes to nothing is answered in words rather than in silence, one sentence per way it can happen, and the row on the panel reads the same three ceilings before it draws, so a press cannot cost more than the label said. The row sits at the foot of the provisions panel rather than on a board of its own, because it is the same decision one rung worse and a captain should meet it while looking at the prices it is worse than, and the note under it names the premium out loud, which is what the plan's iteration clause needs: a captain who cannot see the price cannot be fleeced by it.

There is no switch of its own, and that is the plan's rollback clause read as a rule rather than as an omission. The Barge is the fail safe for a lobby with no Quartermaster, so it cannot be removed while the mode is on; the switch an operator has is the mode's own and the provisions layer under it, and `bargeOn` is the layer's own function rather than a second spelling of its environment value. The state the feature adds is a captain's own, `bargeRound` and `bargeTaken`, which is a per leg tally stamped and reset the way the harbor's rag pile is, plus the two spend counters beside it. The trust boundary is the one every purchase in this build draws: the server never sees the sale and prices nothing. It does see the two counters, because they ride the leg report a client already files, and the reading that trusts them is a measurement rather than a rule.

The page's two numbers are one pass over the records, `readBarge` in `src/lib/game/dashboard.ts`, and the front page tile and the seat panel's first row are handed the same object, so the page cannot print a share the panel beside it disagrees with. The sample is the voyages whose record carries the pair rather than every voyage in the window, because a leg report from before the feature is a voyage this reading was not measuring and counting it as a lobby that avoided the vendor would be the reading flattering itself. The share is taken at each captain's greatest leg rather than summed across their legs, since a leg report is a cumulative figure and adding the legs of one voyage up would count the same Gold once per leg, and the lobbies count is read over voyages rather than over captains, because the plan asks about lobbies. The plan sets no band on the revenue share, so the page prints it as measured and unjudged rather than inventing a threshold nobody wrote, and the number the plan does gate carries its own band. ## The Tag Vocabulary

`src/lib/game/constants/tags.ts` is the closed list and nothing else: the twelve words in the plan's own order, `TagList` as the one tag or two shape, a sentence saying what each one means, and `MAX_TAGS_PER_ENTRY` at two. `src/lib/game/tags.ts` is the reading half: `shippedTagging` walks all five catalogues into one subject, `validateTagging` states the nine things that have to hold and returns every way they do not, and the two readers an effect will ask are `tagsOf`, which answers what one entry carries, and `entriesWithTag`, which gathers everything that carries one. The tags live on the entries rather than in a table beside them: `GOOD_TAGS` is typed as a record over `ITEMS` and sits in the goods module, `FOOD_TAGS` is typed over `FoodId` in the provisions module, every boon and module carries its set beside its id, and each charter's sits on its own record in the difficulty module. Typing the two tables as records over their own catalogue is the first line of the rule rather than a convenience: a good with no row is a compile error in the file that owns it, so the load time check is holding the values an author wrote rather than the rows they might have forgotten.

Nine clauses, and the shape of them is the design. Four are about one entry's own set (one of the twelve, at most two, never none, never twice), two are about the catalogue as a whole (no id twice, every tag carried by something), and three are held to numbers the tree was already keeping somewhere else, which is what makes the vocabulary a description of the content rather than a second opinion about it. Every garment carries `cold`, read against the wardrobe's own table in `./constants/garments`, so the goods that answer a cold leg and the goods a card can name are the same goods by construction. Every food is preserved or perishable and never both, and a preserved food keeps at least as long as a perishable one, read against `FOODS` and its own `keeps` column, with salt fish the row that makes the clause bite because six legs is neither eternal nor quick. A charter carries `armed` exactly when its raid chance steps up and `contraband` exactly when a corrupt broker sails, both biconditionals read off `pirateChance` and `brokerCorruption` on the same record, because the vocabulary's job is to describe the content rather than to allow it. The keeping clause is scoped to the pantry, which is why tea and spices can carry `perishable` as a reading about what a slot is worth while only food has a clock for the clause to measure.

The subject is a parameter rather than read inside, for the reason this tree asks of every detector: a rule that cannot be handed a broken subject is a rule nobody can test. `validateTagging` takes a `TaggingSubject`, which is the entries plus the three facts the cross checks need, and `shippedTagging()` builds the one this tree ships. That is what lets the suite watch each of the nine fire in isolation, against the shipped content with exactly one thing changed and the finding asserted to be the only one, so a clause that fired alongside three others could not pass for the clause under test. The walk reads each row off its own table with no cast, so the only way an entry arrives carrying nothing is from outside the compiler, which is the case the clause about an empty set exists for and the case content read out of a file in G4 will be.

The build runs it. `scripts/tags.ts` validates the shipped content and exits nonzero on any finding, and `npm run build` runs it between `prisma generate` and `next build`, so a good, a card or a charter that breaks the line fails the build rather than the review, which is the plan's own word for the failure mode. On a green run it also prints the distribution, because the shape of the vocabulary is the thing the next balance pass is about to widen: fifty entries across the five catalogues, twelve carrying a pair, and every one of the twelve carried by something, from `public` at nine and `bulk` at nine down to `debt` at one. Nothing about the vocabulary ran in play when it shipped. The tags are static data on records the game already reads, no balance number moved, and no engine function took a tag as an argument: what a captain can do on a voyage was exactly what they could do before, which is the plan's rollback clause, the vocabulary inert until cards reference it. The cards arrived with the card record, which is the next section, and the inertness ended there: the draft's conditions ask tags a question now, and the order board reads the cargo's tags to decide which freight is woven.

Measured on the isolated copy, the whole of it is a build time cost. The walk over all five catalogues is 0.86 microseconds, the nine clauses over the shipped subject are 6.3, and the readers are under a microsecond each at 0.58 for a lookup that finds a good and 0.56 for one that does not, with the tag gathers at 0.76 and 0.72. None of those numbers is on a frame, a request or a socket: the rule's one execution is the build's. What the suite pins is the mechanism those readings are about: thirty one checks in `scripts/smoke/suites/44-tagVocabulary.ts`, the twelve in the plan's order with a meaning each, the walk counted against the catalogues themselves with nothing walked twice and no catalogue empty, the two readers at both of their ends, the cold tag held to the wardrobe, the pantry held to its keepings, the armed tag read across three catalogues at once, and then one check per clause, nine subjects each carrying the one thing wrong that clause is about, from the third tag to the tag nothing carries to the charter that gains teeth without saying so.

## The Card Record

`src/lib/game/constants/cards.ts` is the shape and `src/lib/game/cards.ts` is the pool over it. The shape is one record for boons, modules and charters alike: an identifier, a kind, a power, the tags it carries, the path it leans toward, the trigger that deals it, the condition that decides how likely it is, the effect it has, the modes that offer it, and its strings in both languages. The condition is a closed union of five arms, an always with a weight, a purse below a line, a purse above one, holding so many of a tag, and a roster that can fill the roles the card names, each arm carrying both the weight it takes when it fits and the weight it takes when it does not. The effect is a closed union of two, the round's flags or the hull, which is the whole of what a card can do to a voyage. The pool carries the twenty eight records in `./constants/drafts`, the readers a screen names a card through, the draw, the tally a save keeps and the report that reads it, and `validateCards`, the detector the build runs.

What the record changed in the engine is where a card's behaviour lives. The draft used to hold one arm per card in `./engine/boons`, reading the hold and the roster by name, and it now asks the pool what is on offer for this captain: `offerPool` walks the cards the difficulty tier has unlocked, weights each one by the mode, the condition and the path lean, and keeps the ones above zero. A condition moves a weight rather than deciding whether a card may appear, which is the plan's sentence about a weighted pool rather than a filtered one, and the one card authored at zero for a captain it cannot serve is the exception rather than the rule. The mode field is one number per mode on every card, and classic withholds three cards from its pool (the deep sea escort pact, the tax evasion ledger and Woven Monopoly) while holding every card it keeps to a power of three; Ocean Gambit runs all twenty eight to a ceiling of five. Both weightings are authored on every record, so switching pools is a content change rather than a code change, and `npm run check:cards` prints both pools with their widths.

The evaluation instrument is the pair the plan asked for. The draft writes a card down where it is put in front of a captain and writes it down again where it is taken, both through `noteCardOffer` and `noteCardPick` into the save's tally, and `scripts/cardConversion.ts` prints the offer to pick conversion per card with the appearance count beside it through `readCardConversion`, whose floor of forty appearances is the same number F7 reads a combination at. The validator is the build gate: `npm run build` runs `scripts/cards.ts` between the tag check and `next build`, and it refuses the build on an identifier that is not one, two cards sharing an id, a kind or a trigger outside the closed lists, a power above the mode's ceiling or not a whole number, a tag outside the twelve F1 closed, a path lean that is a lockout rather than a lean, a role the roster cannot hold, an effect that writes no flag or a module that does not work by being installed, and a string that is missing, empty, or names a good in either language. That last clause is the scan F1's evaluation promised: ten of the twenty eight cards carried a good's name in their prose and were rewritten against the vocabulary, so Silk Winds is Weaver's Winds, the silk road monopoly is Woven Monopoly, and Hemp Monopoly is Bulk Monopoly.

Measured on the isolated copy, a draft costs 1.26 microseconds for the pool and 0.17 for the draw of three, against 1.22 and 0.17 in the narrower classic pool, so the mode field is not a measurable cost. The single factor readers are under a microsecond each, at 0.90 for a condition, 0.88 for a whole card's weight, 0.88 to sum a tag across the hold, 0.67 to ask whether a card carries one, 0.64 to ask whether an order's cargo does, and 0.008 for a lookup by id, with the two string readers at 0.009. The build's one execution is 323 microseconds for the validator over the shipped subject and 1.5 for the walk that gathers it, which is the same shape the tag check has: a rule that costs a build a third of a millisecond and costs a voyage nothing. What the suite pins is seventy checks in `scripts/smoke/suites/46-cardRecord.ts`: the ten fields on every record, both strings and the three readers a card is named through, the condition arms one at a time with the numbers the authored cards carry, the lean that weights without filtering, a draw read for determinism, the two drafts walked through the engine from the deal to the pick, the tally and the report and the load that heals it, the tag reads an order board makes, and one check per validator clause against a subject built by hand carrying the single thing wrong that clause is about.

## The Cross Voyage Progression

Every voyage's final Reputation becomes Renown XP multiplied by the difficulty tier. The triangular XP curve means level 2 needs 100, level 3 needs 300, level 4 needs 600, and so on. Seven titles track the climb from Deckhand to Silk Road Sovereign. Renown Level 5 unlocks Broker's Favor. Each level above 1 grants 3 extra starting Gold next voyage, capped at 60.

## The Social Economy

Cross player bartering with Direct Barter Offers, on two surfaces over one board. The Captain's Exchange is open to every captain at any Renown level and nothing on it is rationed. Flexible bartering, reached from the harbor chat, is gated on Renown at both ends rather than one: a captain at level 10 or above may post and accept, with an allowance of one completed trade a voyage and a second from level 15. The allowance is spent by the trade and never by the offer, so a captain may advertise the same intent in several places at once and take whichever answer arrives first, and a completed flexible trade retires every other flexible offer its own poster still had standing. Accepting is never rationed on either surface, so a captain whose own offers have all been taken can still take anyone else's. Financial Aid loans. Backing where a third captain co signs an outstanding loan. Ventures where the harbor pools Gold toward a shared target. Bequest Routing where a bankrupt captain redirects outstanding loans to a still active captain. Harbor Watch where the host mutes one captain's room chat.

## The Harbor Manifest

Eighteen designed harbor systems covering market rhythm, peer economy, identity and long game, trust and safety, accessibility, and reading the room.

Six of the seven systems that were still on the roadmap have shipped:

1. **Partial Sight.** A trusted partner sees a banded range read of another captain's cargo during active play. Pure client side rounding, so it adds no new trust boundary.
2. **Great Houses.** A second identity to argue about, separate from the Renown grind. Pledge to one of three Houses, and a harbor wide standings board ranks them on crowns, voyages and best Reputation. Each House grants one small passive perk from the start of every fresh voyage: a free first artisan, one more cargo lot on the purchase board, or cheaper wages against a higher raid chance.
3. **Ages of the Ledger.** The three peer economy tools take turns in the spotlight. The two week rotation, the banner that announces it, and all three effects are live: a backing pledge pays extra Renown, a completed barter trade lands one extra Reputation, and the Broker's Favor payout cap is raised.
4. **Captain's Rival.** The friend you keep sailing against gets a scoreboard of their own.
5. **Voyage Chronicle.** A voyage becomes a short story a captain can read again later.
6. **Quick Start Match.** A solo captain gets dropped into an open harbor instead of having to go find one.

The seventh, **House Rally**, did not ship. It would have given a harbor where a majority of captains share a pledge a flavor banner and a bonus House Standing at voyage end. It is the one system left on the roadmap.

The **Bilingual Harbor** was built in full and then removed at the owner's request. It does not come back without a fresh owner decision.

## The Room's Width

The game room is an app shell on a wide window: it takes the window's own height and never grows past it, each of its three columns scrolls inside itself, and the row of controls stays pinned under the stage. The width each column is allowed is the part that had to be rebuilt. The two rails were taking 46 percent of a 1440 pixel window (`clamp(220px,22vw,300px)` and `clamp(260px,26vw,360px)`), which left the board 716 pixels, and every board inside it was laid out against the _window_ rather than against the column it was standing in: `md:grid-cols-2 xl:grid-cols-3` on the port exchange meant three columns of 215 pixels in a space that only had room for two, so every card broke its own goods row across lines. Both halves are fixed and both are now rules rather than preferences. The rails are `clamp(216px,19vw,268px)` and `clamp(228px,20vw,288px)`, the room's own cap is 1760 pixels because a board wants more glass than the lobby's list does, and the stage is a CSS container: `@container` on the scroller and an `@` variant on every grid inside it, so a board asks the width it is drawn in. A board in a modal still asks the window, which is what a modal is as wide as. The ladders are one column, two at `@2xl` (672px) and three at `@5xl` (1024px) for the buying boards, and one column then three at `@2xl` for the drafts, because a draft is a comparison and three choices cut to a third of a narrow stage compare worse than three read one after another. The four headline readings in the captain's rail sit two by two at every width, since a cell four across a rail is fifty pixels and the word Reputation is wider than that on its own.

Two smaller consequences came out of the same pass and are worth naming because both were invisible from a desktop. The keyboard legend now travels with the control bar rather than with the board, and is drawn only at or above the breakpoint where the room splits into columns: inside the stage's own scroller it sat exactly one row below the fold on any screen whose board was short enough to fit, so it was clipped on every screen in Classic, and below that breakpoint the four keys it names do not exist. The right rail's roster row gives the captain's name a line of its own and puts the two purse pills beside the phase label under it, because at a 288 pixel rail the name and the pills together spent the name down to one letter and an ellipsis.

Measured on the isolated copy: Gambit at 1440 by 900 runs columns of 268, 820 and 288, and the port exchange deals its cards 381 pixels wide two to a row; at 1280 it is 243, 717 and 256, with 329 pixel cards, and at 1728 it is 268, 1108 and 288 with three columns of 346. Before the pass the same board dealt 215, 177 and 269 pixel cards at those three sizes. Classic needed nothing beyond the container change: its boon draft read `sm:grid-cols-3` and its cards now measure 213, 247 and 288, and its stage stopped scrolling entirely once the legend moved out of it. A 430 pixel phone used to scroll the whole page sideways by 35 pixels, because the control bar's second row of four buttons could not wrap; the page now measures its own width at every size from 430 up to 2560. What the harnesses pin is the shape rather than the numbers: the shell check drives the room at a wide window and at 430 pixels, and a stage probe walks the ladder from 430 to 2560 reading each grid's computed tracks, every element that overflows its own box, and the document's own width against the window.

## The Operator Screens' Width

The console at `/admin` and the balance dashboard at `/admin/balance` draw into columns capped at 1,152 and 1,024 pixels, and both were laid out against the window. The console's register is the clearer failure: eight columns of table came to 1,034 pixels inside a column capped at 976, so at every desktop size the operator had to drag the roster 60 pixels sideways to reach the Actions column, and at 430 pixels the drag was 485, which put the account's own name off screen while the buttons that act on it were on. Folding the joined date into the identity cell takes 75 pixels back and leaves 958, and 60rem is 960, so the threshold is `@min-[60rem]` and it is read off the column rather than the window. Above it the register is a table and below it a card per account, and both carry every fact the other does. The threshold is spelled out at both call sites rather than held in a constant, because Tailwind reads class names as text and a name assembled from a variable is a name it never emits; the two are exact complements, so an edit to one is an edit to the other.

Three measurements decided the rest of it. The first is that `white-space: nowrap` sets a table cell's minimum width to the whole string, so a second line of identity that could not break was setting the floor for the entire table and was what pushed it 38 pixels past its column: the line wraps now, and the table gives the cell whatever is left over. The second is that a viewport breakpoint on the row buttons fired at 1,280 pixels of window while the column stood at 1,024 whatever the window did, so at 1,024 the five labels were dropped with room to spare and past 1,280 they arrived and widened the table past its column; the labels are unconditional now and the presentation is what changes with the width. The third is that a Pill is an `inline-flex` chip and will happily break its own text across two lines inside its rounded background, which is how "no source" came to be a two line lozenge eight pixels taller than the rows around it; the reading table's verdict cell holds its pill on one line.

The reading table's four columns are given shares so that the four panels lay out identically. Measured at the page's own cap of 974 pixels, Reading needs 321, Gate 261 and Verdict 105 on one line, and Window would need 445; all four together are 1,132 in a column of 974. Window is therefore the column that gives, which suits it, because its values are lists of readings by table size rather than numbers. Before the shares were set the same Gate column came out 291 pixels wide on one panel and 135 on the next, so a gate wrapped on one table and sat on one line on the table below it. Measured on the isolated copy, the console drags 0 pixels at 1,024, 1,152, 1,440, 1,920 and 2,560, where it used to drag 60, and the balance dashboard's four front page tiles read the same tracks at 1,024, 1,440 and 2,560. Neither page scrolls sideways at any size from 320 up.

## The Shared Surfaces

A cleanup pass over the tree looked for structure written out more than once and found three clusters, all of them markup rather than logic: no function body in `src/` over 160 characters appears twice, and the repetition that was there was in JSX.

The first was the close button on a dialog. Five dialogs (settings, the guide, the leaderboard, the shortcut help and the Age banner) each carried the same button: the same circle, the same hover, the same icon at the same size, and nothing chosen by the caller but the two words a screen reader reads out. It is `ModalClose` now, in `modal-overlay.tsx` beside the overlay and the card it already belonged with, and the five call sites pass a label and nothing else. That file's own header had said the close button was the caller's business on the grounds that the callers did not agree about it, and the five copies were identical down to the icon's size, which is the answer to that argument: a look written five times is a look that drifts, and the drift is invisible until two dialogs are open side by side.

The second was the row on a path desk. The escort market, the refit bench and the barter board draw what they are offering as the same row, base classes and tint alike, and the tint is the part that had to stop being three copies: an offer of this captain's own is tinted due and everyone else's is left on the panel, so three copies were three chances for a captain's own row to read as their own on one desk and not on the next. `PathDeskRow` carries the row and its three tones, the third of which is the barter board's own and is what an offer aimed at one captain rather than at the table wears. Its companion `JustForChip` is the chip that aim carries on all three desks, and it reads the name off the offer rather than off the reader, so the captain an offer is aimed at and the captain who aimed it read the same words in the same voice. The name is optional because an offer can outlive the captain it named, and the fallback is the phrase the escort row's own sentence already used for that captain.

The third was the dropdown. `Select` already existed with four callers; the manifest audit, the bazaar's desk and the maroon vote had each written the same height, the same radius and the same border out by hand beside it, four pickers between them. They call it now. A component a later caller can miss is a component that has to say where it belongs, so that file's note now names the rule rather than the boards: a dropdown in this game is this component, and a board that needs a different height asks for it in `className`.

Three clusters were looked at and deliberately left alone. The quiet button tint stands on eight surfaces across four files and stays repeated, because the stylesheet's own rule is that the wash class carries no colour and tint stays at the call site, so naming the combination would contradict the block that offers it. The two vote cards, the manifest audit's and the maroon panel's, read alike and are two different mechanics whose differences are the point of the second one. The three inline panel closes differ in padding, in weight and in the margin one of them carries, which is the whole of what a shared component would have to be told.

Nothing about the game changed, and the gate was re-run in full afterwards: typecheck and lint clean, the smoke suite at 1,077 checks with none failing, six browser harnesses at 97 checks with none failing, the production build at 29 routes, and the palette, private information and closed test audits at their standing readings.

## Deployment

The repository carries one host configuration file, `railway.json`, rather than a written guide. It builds the service, starts it, names the healthcheck, and requires a volume at `/app/db`, so a deploy that has nowhere to keep its database fails loudly at startup instead of coming up empty. `requiredMountPath` is what turns a forgotten volume into a failed deploy rather than a silent loss of every account.

The hosted service deliberately runs the development server rather than a production build. `next build` wants more memory than the smaller plans provide, and a build killed halfway is a deploy that never lands, so the file trades first load speed for a deploy that finishes. `README.md` covers the two things the file cannot express on its own: the volume itself, which the platform creates rather than the file, and the build variable that keeps the development dependencies in the image.

## Why It Is State of the Art Already but Kind of Outdated

The engineering and platform are state of the art. Modern stack, deterministic multiplayer, persistent progression, social economy, ledger integrity, designed harbor systems.

The gameplay content is the deliberately preserved verbatim Easy tier of an older single player game. The two richer difficulties reproduce the Standard and Hard essence through the new platform's own levers rather than porting the original's content library. Difficulty balance is still untuned. One of the eighteen designed harbor systems, House Rally, has not been built, and one smaller piece is visible without being live: the Harbor activity feed, which would read from an endpoint that does not exist yet.

## Architectural Strengths to Conserve

The deterministic engine composition. The pure real money math modules. The ready check phase sync protocol. The Ledger Integrity Pass trust model. The shared helper reputation ceiling. The one venture per voyage room wide rule. The first report wins arbitration pattern. The difficulty as single source of truth threaded through every selector. The six phase leg, as one record per phase and one lap per mode. The live reading of hold and purse in every phase, so a mid phase barter changes what a shelf costs before the phase ends. The `_` prefixed transient signal convention for engine to React to socket relay. The normalize on load defensive read pattern. The wholesale replace modifier flags design. The `addOwnedAmount` single mutation path. The harbor pulse lean not shove formula.

## Defect Report and What Happened to It

Earlier in the project a review produced a list of defects and refactoring targets. Almost all of them have since been closed. They are recorded here with their outcome, because a defect list that only ever grows is not a working document.

**Closed.**

The `nextPhase` barter branch used to pass an empty refunds array, so a captain who advanced from the control bar instead of the Bartering panel abandoned every offer they had posted along with its escrow. The refund list is now a parameter, and the control bar forwards the same live list the panel does.

The realtime layer was one 3,097 line function. It is now a composition root over small modules: presence, checkpoint, barter, aid, loans, ventures, chat, conclusion, pulse, objective, docks, surge, rival, quickstart, status, admin, auth, gambit, audit, maroon, reveal, save, telemetry, bazaar, refits, contracts, consent, draft, voyage-log and the inbound frame limit, with the shared types and the composition root itself alongside them. The frames are one to a file under `src/server/realtime/wiring` since the extraction, named by that root in the order a connection takes its shape.

The conclusion could be entered twice for one voyage. `maybeConcludeVoyage` checked whether the room had already been concluded at its door, then read the roster across an await, so two finish reports landing in the same tick both passed that check and both concluded the same voyage: two Chronicle rows per captain, a second crown's worth of Renown, every rival row recorded twice, and two copies of every frame the conclusion ends with. The claim is now taken again immediately before it is made, with nothing yielding in between, so the captain who loses the race reads the set and leaves.

`PublicUser` and `CaptainStatus` were duplicated between the client and the server, and so was the phase order. Both now have one home, in `src/types/realtime/` and `src/lib/game/checkpoint.ts`. [B1] moved the order one field further in, onto the mode record, because the two modes walk the same six phases in different orders and an order is a property of a voyage: `checkpointPhaseOrder` in `src/lib/game/mode.ts` is the one place an order is written, and `src/lib/game/checkpoint.ts` is the one place it is read.

`intelCost` was mutable state mirroring a module flag. It is derived from the equipped modules.

`fireWorker` read the raw `WAGES` table and so ignored both wage surcharges. It now reads `getHireCost`, the same accessor the hire and payroll paths use. Two display sites in the artisan panel had the same habit, quoting the undiscounted list price on the Dismiss button and on the hire roster while the engine charged the discounted wage. Both read the computed wage from the roster now, so the figure a captain budgets against is the figure settlement charges.

`merchantRatingForScore` moved to `constants/reputation.ts`. `Worker.task` is a branded `Product` type instead of a bare string. The always zero `Worker.progress` field is gone. The dead `names` map in `hireWorker` is gone. `modifierFlags` is a `Partial<Record<ModifierKey, number>>` with the legal keys pinned in one place. The `api/route.ts` Hello World stub is deleted. The Welcome screen's numbers derive from the room's difficulty instead of hardcoding the founding trade. The Settlement Force Pay button has its own destructive styling. The Shipyard back buttons call engine functions instead of writing `g.phase` directly.

`escortHired` looked like a write only flag. It is read, by the Settlement screen, which is what it is for: it is a signal from the engine to the interface. That is now stated where the field is declared.

`docs/BUFF_AUDIT.md` produced a second pass over every declared effect, and all of its findings are closed. The two charter boons read one tier agnostic gate between them, and each reads its own tier now. The Silk goods list omitted the two goods whose recipe uses Silk at the same ratio as one it already included. The Settlement panel rebuilt the pirate roll and the escort quote by hand, which drifted from the functions that roll and charge; it calls those functions now. Harbor Pulse divided by a fixed three goods while the harbor trades up to seven, and now divides by the goods actually in the tally. The Purchase reference price skipped two module discounts the counter applies. A failed save load reset the Renown level to 1, and now leaves the captain where they were. Six numbers the interface stated that the game did not are corrected. The three House perks and the three Age effects were written and read by nothing; they are wired in now rather than deleted, because the design was sound and only the connection was missing.

A pledge was stranded when its borrower went bankrupt. The conclusion sweep skipped any loan whose borrower still had a live socket, on the reasoning that a connected borrower might be about to report. A bankrupt captain keeps their socket open to watch the standings, so their loan was never swept and never repaid, and the escrow behind the pledge riding on it was dropped at the next boot. The sweep treats a bankrupt borrower as absent now.

`partialSight.ts` carried a comment asserting a Backing trust gate at Renown level 5 that no code anywhere enforced. The comment states plainly that no such gate has ever existed, and what the module actually reads, which is a captain's Renown and nothing else.

**Kept, deliberately.**

`engine.ts` re exports flat, so any module can import any other. That was weighed and kept. The engine is one small, self contained, pure subsystem, and a deep import graph across it would buy separation that nothing currently needs.

`restartGame` replaces state with `Object.assign(state, fresh)` rather than swapping the object. The reference has to stay stable, and `createInitialGameState` explicitly initializes the transient fields to `undefined` so nothing leaks across a restart. A captain who changes House between voyages cannot keep the old House's perks by accident.

`completeOrder` is still about 130 lines. It is long, but it is a single rule with a single outcome, and splitting it would spread one decision across several functions without making any of them clearer.
